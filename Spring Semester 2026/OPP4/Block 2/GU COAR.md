# Anatomy GU COAR

## The Pelvic Girdle

The pelvis represents a complete bony ring forming a basin in the lower portion of the abdominal cavity. The pelvic inlet and outlet represent important portals for waste elimination, reproduction, gestation and parturition. The dimensions of the pelvic passages are critical landmarks in obstetrics and gynecology. These measurements will be reviewed in this module.
![[Pasted image 20260303170157.png]]
The pelvic girdle is composed of:

1. 2 hip or innominate bones
    - Each innominate bone consists of the pubis, ilium, and ischium
    - Initially the 3 bones are separated by cartilage, but fuse later in adulthood (primarily after puberty)
2. Sacrum
3. Coccyx
![[Pasted image 20260303170204.png]]
The best way to appreciate the anatomical position of the pelvis is to align the ASIS and the anterior margin of the pubic symphysis in a vertical plane. This also aligns the tip of the coccyx and the upper border of the pubic symphysis in a horizontal plane. Therefore, the pubic bone is directed up and back and the sacrum is directed down and forward.

## The Pelvic Brim

- Important obstetric landmark  
- Anatomical landmarks that form a ring around the inner aspect of the pelvic girdle also creating the boundary of the pelvic inlet
- Posteriorly to anteriorly: the sacral promontory->the ala of the sacrum->arcuate line of the ilium->pecten of the pubis->pubic crest->pubic symphysis
- The pelvic brim divides the pelvic girdle into a false or greater pelvis above, and a true or lesser pelvis below the pelvic brim.
![[Pasted image 20260303170211.png]]
**False pelvis**

- Supports and protects the lower abdominal viscera (i.e., ileum, cecum, sigmoid colon)
- During pregnancy, the fetus lies within this region.
- Bordered by the lower lumbar vertebrae posteriorly, the alae of the ilium and iliacus muscle laterally, and the lower anterior abdominal wall anteriorly.

**True pelvis**

- Located between the pelvic inlet (formed by pelvic brim) and the pelvic outlet. Pelvic outlet formed by the pubic arch (the two ischiopubic rami and the pubic symphysis) anteriorly, the ischial tuberosities laterally, and the tip of the coccyx posteriorly
- Contents of the true pelvis include the urinary bladder, internal organs of reproduction, lower end of the GI tract, and the neurovascular structures supplying these organs
![[Pasted image 20260303170218.png]]
### The Bony Pelvis

The bony pelvis or innominate bone is an irregularly shaped structure composed from two hemipelves joined to the sacrum by the fibrous and synovial sacroiliac joint. The hemipelvis is developed from three individual bones Pubis, Ilium and Ischium that have a central ossification site.

### Sacrum and coccyx

The sacrum= the fused product of 5 sacral vertebrae wedged in between the two large innominate bones.

- Anteriorly the bodies of each sacral vertebrae are seen fused together to make one bone
- Laterally there are four ventral sacral foramina that transmit the ventral sacral rami. The fifth ventral ramus passes inferior to the sacrum
- Posteriorly, the sacrum presents three distinct vertical ridges termed the median, intermediate and lateral crests
- The dorsal sacral foramina are located between the intermediate and lateral crests
![[Pasted image 20260303170248.png]]
### Pelvic Diameters

Of obstetric importance when considering the passage of the fetus during birth.

- The birth canal consists of both bony and soft tissues.
- It begins at the level of the pelvic inlet and is therefore a rigid structure. The dimensions of the pelvic inlet are going to determine how easy it is for the head of the fetus to access the birth canal.
- After entering the pelvic inlet, the fetus passes through relatively soft tissues of the cervix and upper vagina. The next major obstacle is the pelvic outlet-a bony rigid structure. Even with pre-birth relaxation of pelvic ligaments to increase flexibility, the dimensions of the pelvic inlet and pelvic outlet can be limiting factors in vaginal deliveries. Obstetricians are able to determine the capacity of the female pelvis for vaginal delivery by measuring certain critical diameters-such as the conjugate (AP) and transverse diameters of the pelvic inlet.
![[Pasted image 20260303170254.png]]
- The **conjugate** or **AP diameter** is the distance between the sacral promontory and the pubic symphysis. Depending upon where on the pubic symphysis the measurement is made, 3 conjugate diameters can be measured

 ![[Pasted image 20260303170300.png]]

- - The **true conjugate diameter** goes from the sacral promontory to the upper part of the pubic symphysis. This is an anatomical conjugate diameter that can only be measured radiographically (probably not a good thing during pregnancy) and is not useful clinically.
    - The **obstetric conjugate diameter** is the shortest distance between the sacral promontory and the back of the pubic symphysis. Therefore, the obstetric conjugate diameter is the **most accurate in determining** the minimum AP diameter of the pelvic inlet. However, it is not measured clinically because of the presence of the bladder and the fact that it is painful to the patient to do this type of measurement via vaginal exam.
    - The **diagonal conjugate diameter** is the distance between the sacral promontory and the inferior or lower margin of the pubic symphysis. The diagonal conjugate diameter is measured clinically by vaginal exam. To measure the diagonal conjugate diameter the clinician inserts one or two fingers into the vagina and directs the tip of the fingers up against the sacral promontory. The base of the index finger is also up against the lower margin of the pubic symphysis. The clinician makes note where at the base of the index finger the pubic symphysis can be felt and will measure that distance. Although the diagonal conjugate does not measure the shortest AP diameter, it is a close approximation and provides useful info to the clinician.
- The **transverse diameter** measures the widest distance between the arcuate lines of the pelvic brim.
- Diameters are also associated with the pelvic outlet.
- The **anatomic AP diameter** is measured from the lower margin of the pubic symphysis to the tip of the coccyx.

The **obstetric AP diameter** extends from the lower margin of the pubic symphysis to the sacrococcygeal joint. The transverse bituberous diameter is measured between the inner surfaces of the ischial tuberosities
![[Pasted image 20260303170315.png]]
- The **Caldwell-Moloy classification** of types of pelves is based on the ratio of the conjugate and the transverse diameters of the pelvic inlet.
- Four categories of pelves are described. Understand that there is tremendous variation in the dimensions of the pelves and this is just a schema to put some organization to describe different pelves.
- The most common type of female pelvis (50%) is the **gynecoid** in which the pelvic inlet is round with a wide transverse diameter.
- The **android pelvis** is typical of males (<30% AFAB) and is characterized by a somewhat triangular inlet with prominent ischial spines. This type of pelvis in females can be problematic in vaginal deliveries.
- The **anthropoid pelvis** (20% AFAB) is characterized by a much shorter transverse diameter and a long conjugate diameter.
- The **platypelloid pelvis** (3% AFAB) is more like a flattened gynecoid with a short conjugate diameter.

### Piriformis (S1-S2)

- Anterior portion of the sacrum in 3 bands that are connected to the portions of bone between the sacral foramina, passes through and fills the greater sciatic foramen and inserts onto the superior portion of the greater trochanter.
- In anatomical position, the piriformis is an external rotator of the hip
- In hip flexion over 90 degrees, the muscle acts as an abductor and internal rotator of the thigh

## Obturator internus (Spinal roots L5-S1)

- Attaches medially from the deep surface of the obturator membrane (a dense, fibrous sheet covering most of the obturator foramen) and the bony margins of the obturator foramen.
- Tapers as it approaches the lesser sciatic foramen and becomes tendinous as it emerges inferiorly to the ischial spine that attaches laterally on the medial aspect of the greater trochanter, just superior to the trochanteric fossa.
- Internal fascia of the obturator internus acts as the attachment site for the levator ani muscle, which forms the support platform for the pelvic viscera.
- Abducts and laterally rotates the thigh.

### Pelvic Diaphragm
![[Pasted image 20260303170329.png]]
The pelvic diaphragm is a flexible fibromuscular sheet anchored to the perineum, the obturator internus muscle and the sacrum.

- Responsible for providing support for the organs in the pelvic basin and can be divided into two muscles, the levator ani and coccygeus.

**Coccygeus (S4-S5)**

- From the ischial spine of the innominate bone and crosses the pelvic outlet to attach to the lower sacrum and the coccyx in its course it is closely applied to the sacrospinous ligament.

**Levator ani (S2-S4)**

**!![[Pasted image 20260303170343.png]]
- Can be further divided into the medial pubococcygeus, lateral and posterior iliococcygeus and medial bands referred to as the puborectalis or "rectal sling".

1. **Iliococcygeus (S2-4)**

- Originates from a tendinous arch (arcus tendineus) that represents a thickening in the fascia of the obturator internus

2. **Pubococcygeus (S3-S4, pudendal branches)**

- The inner aspect of the superior pubic ramus extending from the pubic symphysis laterally to the level of the obturator canal.
- Both iliococcygeus and pubococcygeus attach to the midline, the iliococcygeus inserts into the anococcygeal raphae while the pubococcygeus inserts partially into the anococcygeal raphae and the external rectal sphincters.

3. **Puborectalis (perineal branches S3-S4)**

- Extends forward onto the perineum embedding into the superior fascia of the urogenital diaphragm.

### Innervation of the pelvis

The innervation of the pelvic organs is by the autonomic nervous system, which is a combination of the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions.

#### Sympathetics

- Begin in the lateral horn of the spinal cord at primarily spinal levels Ll-L2
- Preganglionic sympathetic neurons travel through ventral rami into the spinal nerve to the ventral root to enter the sympathetic trunk through a white communicating rami.

From here there are 2 options:

- preganglionic neuron will travel out of the sympathetic ganglion on a **lumbar splanchnic** nerve to enter the **inferior mesenteric ganglion** and **superior hypogastric plexus,** where it will synapse on a postganglionic neuron, and travel down to the pelvic basin.
- preganglionic neuron will travel down the sympathetic trunk to the S2-S4 sympathetic ganglion and exit as **sacral splanchnic** nerves also connecting with the **inferior hypogastric plexus,** synapsing here and continuing to the target organ.

Regardless of where these splanchnic nerves exit, the trunk the origin is always L1-L2.

#### Parasympathetics

- Via **pelvic splanchnic nerves** from spinal cord at the S2-S4 levels
- Exit directly off of the ventral rami of the S2-S4 and similar to the SNS, the pelvic splanchnic nerves will connect with the **inferior hypogastric plexus** and travel to destination where they will synapse on to a mural ganglion on the organ wall.

# Assessment GU COAR

When evaluating a patient for pelvic pain it is first necessary to determine if the patient is **stable vs. unstable** to determine the next course of action (send to ER). Then when narrowing your differential diagnosis, it is important to define **acute pelvic pain vs. chronic pelvic pain** as this will guide your diagnosis and treatment. 

**Acute Pelvic Pain**: generally defined as lower abdominal or pelvic pain that has lasted less than three months in a non-pregnant adult assigned female at birth (AFAB). This is often a common complaint.

The differential diagnosis includes gynecological, urological, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, vascular and metabolic disorders.

In a patient with acute pelvic pain, it is critical to determine if the patient is stable or unstable. There are several etiologies of acute pelvic pain that are emergencies and can be life threatening, including:

**In Assigned Female at Birth (AFAB) individuals:**

Genitourinary

- Ectopic Pregnancy
- Ovarian Torsion
- Septic Abortion
- Tubo-ovarian abscess
- Ruptured Hemorrhagic Ovarian Cyst

Obstetrical

- Uterine Rupture
- Placental Abruption

**In Assigned Male at Birth (AMAB) individuals:** 

- Testicular Torsion

**Other**

- Appendicitis
- Diverticular disease
- Incarcerated Hernia
- Nephrolithiasis (infected)

When considering the etiology of pelvic pain, it is important to review the causes of pain in the viscera-visceral pain receptors which are located in the serious surfaces, within the mesentery and within the walls of the hollow viscera. Examples of visceral sources of pain include:

- Distention of viscera (ex. Intestines, bladder, uterus, etc)
- Inflammation/Infection of intestinal muscle (fibers)
- Spasm of intestinal muscle (fibers)

True visceral pain is often described as deep, dull and poorly localized. Pain from pelvic viscera can often be perceived in the periumbilical area. Referred visceral pain is characterized by pain felt in the somatic area of the neurologically related viscera (viscerosomatics reflexes).

Although not clearly defined, **Chronic pelvic pain (CPP)** is usually defined by non-cyclic pain perceived in the pelvic region for three to six months (or longer), not related to pregnancy. This pain is unrelated to menses, pain related to menses is defined as dysmenorrhea (a condition characterized by painful or uncomfortable menstrual cramps). Chronic pelvic pain is limited to the anatomic pelvis (the pelvic girdle). Perineal or vulvar pain is usually categorized separately.

Women with CPP may have more than one etiology for the pain, the goals of treatment are focused around identifying any treatable causes. The presence of female pelvic organs makes CPP more common in women. Women who present with pelvic pain may also report pain that radiates outside the pelvis. Associated symptoms include:

- Urinary
- Gastrointestinal
- Altered quality of life
- Mental health changes

Remember that AMAB can get pelvic pain as well. In an AMAB with pelvic and low back pain it would be important to consider prostatitis or prostate cancer. A digital rectal exam (DRE), urinalysis/urine culture, PSA and possibly imaging (x-rays, CT abd/pelvis, etc) or biopsy would be warranted.

# Osteopathic Considerations (ABCS) GU COAR

Remember that we treat _people_ in osteopathic medicine, not clinical conditions. Therefore, there is no specific technique for a specific diagnosis including pelvic pain. Each patient, regardless of the clinical diagnosis, requires a structural diagnosis in order to determine what treatment best suits their clinical presentation. There is no algorithm for the osteopathic treatment of patients- no one person will have the same constellation of somatic dysfunction. We can, however, consider each patient in the context of the A (Autonomics), B (Biomechanics), C (Circulation), S (Screening) as a way to begin to discover where we may want to aim our diagnosis and treatment.

**A (Autonomics)**

Sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation is received by each organ. Often thought to be antagonistic, they work in coordination with each other to regulate organ function in a balanced and homeostatic way.

The **sympathetic system** has two major components: vascular and visceral.

1. Vascular associated with spinal nerves and innervates fascia, smooth muscle of vasculature, smooth muscle of hair follicles, secretory cells in the sweat glands of the skin.
2. Visceral component innervates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, nodular tissue and glandular organs of the thoracic, abdominal, pelvic and perineal viscera.

The **parasympathetic system** innervates only visceral organs in the head, neck, thorax, abdomen and pelvis and is divided into two portions: cranial and sacral.

# Osteopathic Considerations GU COAR

**What effects would the parasympathetic and sympathetic nervous system have on the uterus? How about the testes?**

- **Sympathetic**
    - Uterus/Cervix
        - T10-L2
        - Adrenergic fibers that end of the vasculature and in the smooth muscle of the myometrium and glands of endometrium
    - Prostate
        - T11-L2
        - Activation of smooth muscles during ejaculation to create anterograde movement
    - Testes/Ovaries
        - T10-T11
        - Initiate strong peristaltic waves in the vas deferens for the propulsion of sperm
        - Adrenergic fibers around blood vessels, interstitial cells of seminiferous tubules and in the tubules of the epididymis
    - Penis/Clitoris
        - T11-L2
        - Vasoconstriction and loss of erection
-  **Parasympathetic**
    - Uterus/Cervix
        - S2-S4
        - Few in the circular muscle layer of the myometrium
        - Mostly located in the cervix; cervical muscle contraction and helps keep the cervix closed
    - Prostate
        - S2-S4
        - Prostatic secretion
    - Testes/Ovaries
        - S2-S4
        - Less dense than sympathetic fibers, cholinergic and targets the vas deferens and the seminal vesicles
    - Penis/Clitoris
        - S2-S4
        - Cholinergic fibers release nitric oxide relaxing blood vessel walls and increasing perfusion allowing for erectile tissue to become engorged
