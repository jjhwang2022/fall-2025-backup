# Clinical Skills HA COAR

Everyone performs their integrated physical exam slightly differently. Some of you may wish to separate the HEENT exam from the CN exam, while others will choose to integrate it together as noted below.  While there is no correct answer, it is important to practice and develop your routine because if you perform your physical exam the same way each time then you are less likely to miss things!

**Anterior Seated**

**Head and Neck**

- Observe: General posture (including shoulders) and head carriage, facial symmetry
- Inspect: scalp, skull, and hair for symmetry, trauma, or lesions; AROM of cervical spine
- Palpate: face, suboccipital muscles and neck for muscle hypertonicity, lymphadenopathy, trachea, Chapman’s points, sinus pressure tenderness; palpation of supraorbital, infraorbital, and mental foramen
- TMJ palpation, jaw opening/closing, palpation of temporal region and mastoid
- CN Exam: Facial sensation/jaw movements/corneal reflexes (CN V), facial symmetry and strength (CN VII)

 **Eyes**

- Observe: symmetry, pupil size, erythema
- Inspect: conjunctiva and sclera
- Pupillary reaction and accommodation (direct and consensual response) (CN II/III)
- Fundoscopic exam for papilledema
- Visual acuity and visual field testing (CN II)
- CN Exam: EOM (CN III, IV, VI), check for nystagmus

**Ears**

- Observe: ear position
- Inspect: external ear and external auditory canal
- Otoscopic Exam: auditory canal and tympanic membranes
- Palpate: mastoid process, temporal region
- Auditory acuity (whispered voice test)
- CN Exam: hearing (CN VIII)

**Nose**

- Observe: symmetry, nasal septum, nasal mucosa
- Inspect: nares with otoscope
- CN Exam: smell (CN I)

**Throat (Mouth and Pharynx)**

- Observe: lips and muscular folds around the mouth, oral mucosa
- Inspect: tongue, palate, oropharynx, dentition, tonsils
- Palpate: temporomandibular joint for clicks or deviation (if not already done above)
- CN Exam: bite (CN V), tongue symmetry/position/movement (CN XII), uvula deviation (CN X), swallowing and rise of the palate/gag reflex (CN IX, X)

**Neuro**

- Observe: gait (tandem walk, heel/toe), speech
- Inspect: muscle strength, muscle bulk, muscle tone, coordination, rapid alternating movement, sensation, proprioception, standing flexion test
- Deep tendon reflexes: upper and lower extremities
- Plantar response (Babinski), , Pronator Drift, Hoffman reflex
- CN Exam: voice and speech (CN V, VII, X, XII), shoulder and neck movements (CN XI)

**Posterior Seated**

- Observe: position of the thorax and cervical spine, cervical AROM
- Palpate: thyroid, swallow, cervical PROM, paraspinals, scalenes, SCM, upper trapezius, Zink Screen- O/A, C/T, T/L, L/S junctions, thoracic spine (short lever) and TART
- Perform: auditory acuity

**Standing**

- Observe gait (tandem walk, walking on heels, walking on toes) general posture
- Observe: standing flexion test if time permits
- Perform: Romberg test

**Supine**

- Observe: neck mobility, nuchal rigidity (Brudzinski sign, Kernig sign)
- Palpate: cranial motion- vault contact, static symmetry, dynamic evaluation, SBS motion, temporal bone motion, parietal bone motion, occipitomastoid suture, anterior and posterior cervical counterstrain points, sacral motion

# Anatomy HA COAR

**What causes the pain of a headache? The brain itself does not have nociceptive fibers, so when there is a headache, it is due to nociception of other structures in the head with nociceptive input.**

- **Extracranially**:
    - Scalp
    - Arteries
    - Muscle
    - Mucous membranes
    - External auditory meatus
    - Teeth
- **Intracranially**:
    - Meninges
    - Venous sinuses
    - Arteries
- **Other structures** innervated by C1, C2, C3 can also contribute to headache pain either through innervation or referral patterns
    - Suboccipital muscles
    - Muscles of the neck
    - Zygapophysial joints of C2-C4
    - OA (C1) and AA (C2)
    - Dura of the spinal cord, posterior fossa, and inferior surface of the tentorium cerebelli
    - Vertebral artery
    - Trapezius and SCM

**Innervation considerations:**

**_Ventral and Dorsal Cervical Plexus_**

The ventral cervical plexus arises from spinal nerves C1-C4 and provides sensory innervation to the neck and head as well as motor innervation to the musculature of the anterior neck.  The sensory component of this plexus will exit posterior to the sternocleidomastoid muscle and split into 4 major named nerves and dural branches:

- **_Lesser occipital n. C2_** - Sensory to skin of the lateral head posterior to the ear
- **_Great auricular n. C2, C3_** - Sensory to the inferior ear, skin over the parotid gland and mastoid process
- **_Transverse cervical n. C2 ,C3_** - Sensory to the entirety of the anterior neck (passes deep to the platysma and sends sensory branches through the muscle.
- **_Supraclavicular n. C3, C4_** - Divides into medial, intermediate and lateral branches to supply sensory innervation over the clavicle, pectoralis major, deltoid and parts of the posterior shoulder.
- **_Dural branches_** - Nerves from the C2, C3 spinal nerves will ascend through the foramen magnum, but also travel with the vagus nerve and hypoglossal nerve to innervate the dura mater of the posterior cranial fossa.

Motor innervation is transmitted through the ansa cervicalis (originates from superior (C1-C2) and inferior (C2-C3) roots of cervical plexus) and will control the infrahyoid muscles (excluding thyrohyoid).  Additionally, fibers from the ventral cervical rami will form the phrenic nerve (C3-C5) and will innervate the anterior, middle and posterior scalenes (C4-C6) , longus colli/capitis, and along with CN X1, the trapezius and sternocleidomastoid muscles. 
![[Pasted image 20250924122454.png]]
The dorsal cervical plexus (Plexus of Cruveilhier) receives contributions from the dorsal rami of C1-C3 and will distally form its own nerve. Like the ventral plexus, all three nerves have connecting fibers with each other.  In addition to the following, the dorsal rami of the dorsal cervical plexus innervates the surrounding ligaments of the cervical spine, the facet (zygapophyseal) joints segmentally and the spinal dura. 

- **_Suboccipital n. C1_** - Motor innervation to suboccipital muscles: rectus capitis posterior major, rectus capitis posterior major, obliquus capitis superior and obliquus capitis inferior. C1 has no cutaneous nerve branches.
- **_Greater occipital n. C2_** - Sensory to the posterior head. Motor to posterior neck musculature: splenius capitis, semispinalis capitis. The greater occipital nerve passes directly through the semispinalis capitis muscle.
- **_3rd occipital n. C3_** - Provides sensory innervation to the posterior neck and lower part of the head.

**Trigeminal Nerve - CN V**

The trigeminal nerve is the most widespread cranial nerve in the head and neck, providing sensory innervation to the face as well as motor innervation to the muscles of mastication and serves as a conduit for sympathetic neurons to the head.  Three large areas of the face can be mapped out as peripheral nerve regions indicated by the three divisions of the trigeminal nerve: Ophthalmic V1, Maxillary V2 and Mandibular V3.

**_Ophthalmic Nerve V1_** -  Sensory only - The ophthalmic nerve exits the cranium through the superior orbital fissure and divides into 3 main branches:

- **_Lacrimal n._** - Sensory to the lacrimal gland, conjunctiva, and upper eyelids. Carries parasympathetic innervation to the lacrimal gland and mucous membranes. 
- **_Frontal n._** - Divides into supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves to provide sensory innervation to the forehead and scalp
- **_Nasociliary n_**. - Sensory to the proximal part of the nose. The nasociliary nerve contains autonomic fibers for the mucous membranes and eye.
- **_Dural branches_** - Sensory to anterior dura and supratentorial dura mater
![[Pasted image 20250924122526.png]]
**_Maxillary nerve  V2_** - Sensory only - The maxillary division exits the cranium through the foramen rotundum of the sphenoid bone and divides into many branches.  Only a few will be discussed here. 

- **_Zygomatic n._** - Sensory innervation to the temple and cheek, forms an anastomosis with the zygomatic branch of the facial nerve CN VII
- **_Infraorbital n_**. - Exits the infraorbital foramen of the maxillary bone and provides sensory innervation to the nose, cheek and upper lip. The infraorbital nerve also forms an anastomosis with facial nerve branches.
- **_Superior alveolar n_**. - Sensory to the maxillary sinus, palate, and maxillary teeth.
- **_Dural branches_** - Sensory innervation to the dura in the anterior and middle cranial fossa.
![[Pasted image 20250924122533.png]]
**_Mandibular nerve V3_** - Sensory and motor - The mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve exits the skull through the foramen ovale of the greater wing of the sphenoid bone and immediately divides into 4 major branches. The mandibular division is associated with two parasympathetic ganglia:  otic and submandibular.

- **_Buccal n_**. - Sensory innervation to the skin over the buccinator muscle.  Forms an anastomosis with the buccal branches of the facial nerve.
- **_Lingual n._** - Sensory to the anterior two-thirds of the tongue and floor of the mouth. Carries taste to the anterior two-thirds of the tongue via the chordae tympani of the facial nerve.
- **_Auriculotemporal n_**. - Emerges onto the face near the temporomandibular joint and provides sensory innervation to the joint and skin around the tragus of the ear, auricle, and the temporal region. Forms an anastomosis with the zygomatic and temporal branches of the facial nerve. 
- **_Inferior alveolar n_**. - Travels over the lateral pterygoid muscle and gives off the nerve to mylohyoid, which innervates the mylohyoid and anterior digastric muscles before entering the mandibular foramen. Upon entering the mandible, the nerve provides sensory innervation to the mandibular teeth before exiting the mandible through the mental foramen as the mental nerve.  The mental nerve provides cutaneous innervation to the chin and lower lip. 
- **_Dural branches_** - Sensory innervation to the dura of the middle and inferior cranial fossa.
![[Pasted image 20250924122541.png]]The motor division of the trigeminal nerve will provide innervation to the muscles of mastication: temporalis, masseter, lateral and medial pterygoid muscles.  Additionally, V3 will innervate the tensor tympani, tensor veli palatini, mylohyoid, and the anterior digastric muscles. 

**_Vagus Nerve - CN X_**

The vagus nerve is one of the most extensive nerves in the body providing parasympathetic innervation to the thorax and abdomen; but for our purposes here we will only discuss its distribution in the head.   The vagus nerve, after passing through the jugular foramen, will give off two branches to the cranium.

- **_Meningeal/dural branches_** - Provides sensory innervation to the dura mater of the posterior cranial fossa.
- **_Auricular branch_** - Also known as Arnold’s nerve, this branch joins with a nerve from the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) and provides an ascending branch to the facial nerve. The auricular branch supplies sensory innervation to the skin of part of the ear and the external auditory meatus.

**Arterial Blood Flow in the Neck and Cranium**

Blood from the heart is transported to the head and neck via bilateral carotid and vertebral arteries. The carotid will bifurcate into the external and internal carotid arteries round the C3-C4 vertebral level.  The internal will terminate at the Circle of Willis to supply the brain and the external will supply the neck and face. Lumbar punctures (LP) collect cerebrospinal fluid for analysis if there is clinical suspicion of subarachnoid hemorrhage, infectious or inflammatory etiology of headache.  The most common use of the LP is to diagnose or exclude meningitis in patients presenting with some combination of fever, altered mental status, headache, or meningeal signs. Examination of the CSF has a high sensitivity and specificity for determining the presence of bacterial and fungal meningitis.

The findings on CSF analysis also may help distinguish bacterial meningitis from viral infections of the central nervous system. However, there is often substantial overlap.

Nonurgent — A nonurgent LP is indicated in the diagnosis of the following conditions. The findings are discussed in the appropriate topic reviews:

- Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor cerebri)
- Carcinomatous meningitis
- Tuberculous meningitis
- Normal pressure hydrocephalus
- CNS syphilis
- CNS vasculitis

Conditions in which LP is rarely diagnostic but still useful include:

- Multiple sclerosis
- Guillain-Barré syndrome
- Paraneoplastic syndromes

LP is also required as a therapeutic or diagnostic maneuver in the following situations:

- Spinal anesthesia
- Intrathecal administration of chemotherapy
- Intrathecal administration of antibiotics
- Injection of contrast media for myelography or for cisternography

**CONTRAINDICATIONS** — Although there are no absolute contraindications to performing the procedure, caution should be used in patients with:

- Possible raised intracranial pressure
- Thrombocytopenia or other bleeding diathesis (including ongoing anticoagulant therapy)
- Suspected spinal epidural abscess

**COMPLICATIONS** — LP is a relatively safe procedure, but minor and major complications can occur even when standard infection control measures and good technique are used. These complications include:

- Post-LP headache
- Infection
- Bleeding
- Cerebral herniation
- Minor neurologic symptoms such as radicular pain or numbness
- Late onset of epidermoid tumors of the thecal sac
- Back pain

Per UpToDate:

Migraine with aura is a headache disorder characterized by attacks of transient neurologic symptoms usually followed by headaches. Those symptoms last for 2-72 hours. A typical migraine attack progresses through 4 phases: the prodrome, the aura, the headache, and the postdrome.

The aura of migraine usually develops gradually over more than five minutes. Less often, the aura develops more acutely (ie, in less than five minutes). The acute onset of aura makes confusion with a transient ischemic attack (TIA) or stroke more likely. In one case series, four patients (2 percent) had exclusively acute-onset visual aura.

Visual aura — A visual aura classically begins as a small area of visual loss often just lateral to the point of visual fixation. It may either appear as a bright spot or as an area of visual loss. Over the following five minutes to one hour, the visual disturbance often expands to involve a quadrant or hemifield of vision. Along the expanding margin, geometric shapes or zigzagging lines often appear. The shapes account for one of the common names for aura, the "fortification spectrum," because of the resemblance of the aura to the walls of a medieval fortress. The positive visual phenomena may assume a sickle or C-shape, expanding over time toward the peripheral visual field, leaving a scotoma or area of complete visual loss in their wake. As the aura moves off into the peripheral visual field, it often assumes a shimmering or scintillating quality. As the aura resolves, vision usually returns first to the areas of central vision initially affected by the aura.

Sensory aura — The sensory aura is also common and typically follows the visual aura within minutes, although it may also occur without the visual aura. A sensory aura usually begins as a tingling in one limb or on one side of the face. As the tingling sensation migrates across one side of the face or down the limb, numbness is left in its wake that may last up to an hour. The sensory aura may also move inside the mouth, affecting the buccal mucosa and half the tongue. The slow spread of positive symptoms (scintillations or tingling) followed by negative symptoms (scotoma or numbness) is quite characteristic of migraine aura and is not typical for an ischemic event.

Language aura — Less common than the visual and sensory auras is the language or dysphasic aura. Language auras cause transient problems that may run the gamut from mild wording difficulties to frank dysphasia with paraphasic errors.

Motor aura — In the rarest of auras, motor aura, the limbs and possibly the face on one side of the body become weak. Because of information related to the genetic basis of the motor aura, it has been separated from the other forms of aura and classified as **hemiplegic migraine**. The aura symptoms may occur either singly or in sequence, but they do not generally occur simultaneously.

Aura without headache — Some patients may experience aura without an associated headache. Migraine aura without headache (also known as migraine equivalent and acephalgic migraine) manifests as isolated aura unaccompanied by headache. In a Danish case study, 38 percent of patients reported having both attacks of migraine aura without headache as well as migraine aura with headache, and 4 percent had exclusively migraine aura without headache.

Auras without headache are more common in individuals aged >50 years old who have a previous history of migraine with aura and were formerly called late-life migraine accompaniments. The most common symptoms are visual auras, followed by sensory auras (paresthesia), speech disturbances, and motor auras (weakness or paralysis). The most common presentation is a gradual evolution of aura symptoms with spread of transient neurologic deficits over several minutes and serial progression from one symptom to another. These events may be confused with transient ischemic attacks.

Hemiplegic migraine — The primary feature that separates hemiplegic migraine from other types of migraine with aura is the presence of motor weakness as a key manifestation of aura in the attacks. In addition to **weakness**, the aura in hemiplegic migraine also includes visual, sensory, and/or language disturbance. Symptoms associated with migraine with brainstem aura such as vertigo or diminished consciousness may also occur in some patients. Some patients report prolonged auras lasting several hours. Most patients report headache with or after onset of the aura. Attacks can recur for many years, but overall prognosis is favorable with waning frequency of attacks over time. Hemiplegic migraine may occur as a familial or sporadic form.

# Pathophysiology, Classification HA COAR

**Migraine Management**

**Pathophysiology**

Patients with migraine are likely to be vulnerable to outside triggers due to mutations in the regulation of ion balance (e.g. mutations in calcium channels subtypes and Na+/K+ ATPase subunits) in neurons. The primary neuronal dysfunction leads to a sequence of changes intracranially and extracranially that account for migrainous symptoms, including the three phases of premonitory (prodrome), aura, and headache symptoms.

![[Pasted image 20250924124804.png]]
Migraine headaches are typically activated upon environmental triggers that produce changes deep within the brain.

**Prodrome:** Once initiated a cascade of central cortical depression and reduced blood flow (oligemia) spreads slowly throughout the cortex.

**Aura:** Changes in nerve cell activity and blood flow, associated with a brief wave of hyperemia followed by prolonged oligemia may (18%-31% of patients with migraine) result in producing visual disturbance, numbness, or tingling, and dizziness depending on which region of the cortex is most affected.

**Headache Pain**: Pain results from a combination of cranial blood vessel dilation, trigeminal innervation of the blood vessels, and the reflex connections from the trigeminal system with cranial parasympathetic outflow.  Central activation of parasympathetic outflow (see green neuron and pterygopalantine ganglion below) produces vascular inflammation in blood vessels of the dura. The inflammation, in turn, stimulates the trigeminal nerve (see yellow and trigeminal ganglion below) that relays nociceptive inputs to the spinal cord resulting in a severe or throbbing pain sensation. In particular, the involvement of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve and its overlap with layers in the C1 and C2 in the spinal cord produce a type of referred pain that is perceived to be in the frontal and temporal region commonly found in the migrainous headache (see inset). This process can cycle for 4-72 hours.

- **Neurovascular-Reflex key neurotransmitters**: Stimulation of the trigeminal ganglion results in release of vasoactive neuropeptides, including serotonin, dopamine, substance P, calcitonin gene-related peptide, and neurokinin A. Release of these neuropeptides results in the process of neurogenic inflammation. The two main components of this sterile inflammatory process are vasodilation and plasma protein extravasation. Neurogenic inflammation is responsible for the pain of migraine. Elevated levels of vasoactive neuropeptides have been found in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with chronic migraine, suggesting chronic activation of the trigeminovascular system in these patients. (Neurogenic inflammation may lead to the process of sensitization. Goadsby, PJ, Lipton RB, Ferrari, MD. Migraine. Current Understanding and Treatment. _N Engl J Med_2002; 346:257-270).
- **Central Neurotransmitter Pathways involved in migraine features**: The symptoms of the migraineur are likely to involve both the serotonergic and dopaminergic pathways in the brain. Peripheral serotonergic pathways that involve the vasculature are important in the cascade of events that produce migraine attacks. In addition, serotonin, released from brainstem nuclei may underlie the migraine symptoms that involve mood and appetite. Data also support a role for dopamine in the pathophysiology of certain subtypes of migraine. For example, migrainous attacks can be induced by dopaminergic stimulation. Moreover, there is dopamine receptor hypersensitivity in migraineurs that may be exhibited via yawning, nausea and vomiting, and hypotension. Dopamine receptor antagonists are effective therapeutic agents in migraine management, especially when given parenterally or concurrently with other antimigraine agents. (Funct Neurol. 2000;15 Suppl 3:171-81)

**Acute treatment of symptoms** --involves a nonspecific decrease in nociception via NSAIDS/Aspirin, acetaminophen and opioids. Of these the safest options are the non-opioid pain relievers. The more specific drugs (a class of drugs called triptans) revolve around suppressing the vascular inflammation and the concomitant activation of Trigeminal neuronal afferents. This later class of drugs represents the most effective pharmacologic treatment option. In addition, if vomiting and nausea are present anti-nausea drugs can be given concomitantly.

The figure below demonstrates the target areas for the most common drugs used to treat acute migraine headaches (NSAIDS and Triptans). NSAIDS and aspirin can suppress the production of vasodilatory prostaglandins PGE2 and PGI2 that when made can increase vascular inflammation. In addition, prostaglandins can activate neurons in the trigeminal ganglion. Triptans are a class of drugs that are selective agonist for serotonin 5-HT1D and 5-HT1B receptors. These receptors play two vital roles in the process of migraine headaches. 1) Activation of 5-HT1B receptors produces vasoconstriction thus opposing the vasodilatory effects on dura blood vessels. 2) Activation of 5-HT1D receptors reducing the ability of afferent sensory fibers from firing. This results in less activation of trigeminal ganglion. These receptors play a vital role in the pathophysiology of migraine headaches and why they are considered a specific treatment for this disease.

# Osteopathic Considerations (ABCS) HA COAR

What are _relative_ contraindications to treating patients with osteopathic cranial manipulative medicine?

- Acute or recent CVA, TIA, or hemorrhage
- Acute or unstable head trauma
- Acute skull fracture
- Acute concussion (mTBI)

Remember that we treat _people_ in osteopathic medicine, not clinical conditions. Therefore, there is no specific technique for a specific diagnosis including headache. Each patient, regardless of the clinical diagnosis requires a structural diagnosis within the context of their clinical history to determine what treatment best suits their clinical presentation.  There is no algorithm for the osteopathic treatment of patients- no one person will have the same constellation of musculoskeletal abnormalities.  We can, however, consider each patient in the context of the A (Autonomics), B (Biomechanics), C (Circulation), S (Screening) to begin to discover where we may want to aim our diagnosis and treatment.

**Further reading: FOM 4th edition Chapter 43D: Osteopathic Considerations in Neurology pg 1258**

- **A (Autonomics)**
    - **_Sympathetics_**
        - Originate at T1-T4- travel to the superior cervical ganglia which lies anterior to the C2 transverse process. Sympathetic fibers then travel along the internal carotid, the external carotid and cranial nerves II, III, IV, VI, and IX
    - **_Parasympathetics_**
        - Begin at 4 nuclei in the brainstem and are associated with CN III, VII, IX, X
        - They synapse in a ganglia- either the ciliary, sphenopalatine, submaxillary, or otic
        - “When evaluating patients with migraine, pay attention to the cranium, in particular the sphenobasilar area, the temporal area, the sphenosquamous pivot, and the jugular foramen. Nausea and vomiting with migraine may be due to vagal involvement at the jugular foramen accompanied by tension on the dural sleeve. Also consider the suboccipital region, C2 (vagus involvement), and the upper thoracic spine and the associated ribs” [Richards].

- **B (Biomechanics)**
    - **_Head-_** remember to evaluate for static and dynamic findings as well as the motion of the SBS (sphenobasilar synchondrosis), rate of the CRI, evaluation of the TMJ, temporalis, masseter, and pterygoids (as bruxism and TMJ syndrome/TMD can contribute to headaches)
    - **_Cervical Spine_**- cervical spine mechanics, fascia, and muscles may have an effect on the greater and lesser occipital nerve or refer pain themselves (sclerotomal or referral pain patterns)
    - **_Thoracic Spine-_** as it connects through fascial and muscular connections and importance of addressing sympathetic tone (as above)
    - **_Sacrum/Pelvis_**- don’t forget the link between the cranium and the sacrum through the dural attachments (reciprocal tension membrane). The sacrum should be able to move with respiration (breathing)-flexion and extension, as well as with the CRI with cranial counternutation and nutation.

- **C (Circulation)**
    - **_Tentorium Cerebelli_**- dural membrane that is considered a diaphragm and if restricted may impede CSF flow
    - **_Thoracic inlet_**- lymphatic and vascular drainage from the head may be impeded here
    - **_Diaphragm_**- major lymph/venous pump

- **S (Screening)**
    - **_Postural issues_**- upper crossed syndrome (to be reviewed later this semester), forward head/neck posture, scoliosis. Remember that abnormal tensions through muscles and fascia distant from the head can still have an effect.
    - **_Leg length discrepancy_**- or sacral base unleveling can cause compensatory postures all the way up

**Occipitomastoid (OM) Suture Release** may assist with venous drainage from the cranium as well as assist in restoring motion to the occiput and temporal bone. Recall that CSF drains through arachnoid granulations into the venous sinuses. Where does it go from there? The venous sinuses ultimately drain from the sigmoid sinus into the internal jugular vein which passes through the jugular foramen. The jugular foramen is anteromedial to the OM suture.

- Make an accurate clinical diagnosis

    - Treat what you find!

Key things to consider when treating with OMT for a patient with a migraine:

- Free the sphenobasilar and temporal regions.

- Use any procedure to normalize the function of the thoracic and rib dysfunctions.
- Treat counterstrain points in the suboccipital area and the inion point
