# History LBP COAR

**Location:** Outpatient clinic

**CC**: Low Back Pain

**HPI**: A 48-year-old male presents with acute onset of low back pain 3 days ago.  He was playing basketball with his son, bent over to get the ball, and felt a strong “pull.”  He had immediate “crippling” pain, worse on the right side of his low back and radiating into his buttock and posterior upper thigh region. Initially it was a 10/10 pain; currently it is a 7/10 pain and at minimum 5/10. It is made better with a heat pack and ibuprofen. It worsens with standing straight up, sitting, and lying in certain positions.  He has had trouble sleeping due to the pain and was out of work the first two days.  He returned to work today, but states that it was very uncomfortable.  He admits to some numbness and tingling in the right buttock region and into the posterior thigh, but denies weakness or changes in bowel or bladder function.  He has a history of mild chronic low back pain, but it has never been this bad before.  He had an x-ray of his lumbar spine 5 years ago and was told that it was normal.  He has never used prescribed medications, PT, OMT, or further imaging for his back pain.  Usually, he copes with it by utilizing ibuprofen and heat.  He has otherwise been in his usual state of health without recent illness or injury.

**PMHX**: elevated cholesterol, HTN

**PSHX**: Right ACL repair at age 18, appendectomy at age 24

**Trauma history**: Soccer injury senior year of high school leading to ACL repair surgery, several MVCs, several sprained ankles during 4 years of soccer in high school

**Allergies**: NKDA, NKFA, no latex allergies

**Medications**: Atorvastatin 20 mg QD, Lisinopril 20mg QD, ibuprofen 400-800 mg 1-3 times a day as needed for back pain

**Social History**: admits to smoking 1½  a pack per day since age 18. Denies illicit drugs. Has occasional/social EtOH. He is married, and in a monogamous relationship. He has 2 children, ages 18 and 16. He works as an insurance agent - sits most of the day. He walks the dog daily 1-2 miles and attempts to run and weightlift on the weekends

**Family History**: Mother – alive, 68: HTN, osteoporosis; Father – alive, 70: osteoarthritis (knees, back), HTN, CVA; Brother – alive, 45: HTN; children are healthy

**Patient Concerns**: “I am worried I have a herniated disc - do I need to get an MRI?”

**ROS:**

**General:** denies fevers, chills, night sweats, fatigue, or weight changes

**HEENT:** denies sore throat, rhinorrhea, congestion, sinusitis, vertigo, jaw pain, bruxism, seasonal allergies

**Resp:** denies cough, shortness of breath

**Cardiac:** denies chest pain, palpitations

**Neuro**: denies muscle weakness, seizure, loss of consciousness, ataxia, vision changes.  +numbness, +tingling right buttock and posterior thigh

**MSK**: LBP as per HPI, chronic right knee pain - aching and sore with increased activity. Denies other arthralgias, myalgias, joint swelling or joint redness.

**Derm**: denies rash or skin changes

# Integrated Exam for LBP

**Integrated MSK, ortho, abdominal, and osteopathic structural exams**

**Standing**

- Observe general posture, spinal curves, alignment
- Observe gait (foot drop, walking on heels (L4), walking on toes (S1), pronation, supination, LE abnormalities during gait)
- Perform: standing flexion test

**Anterior Seated**

**Neuro exam**

- LE Reflexes - Achilles (S1), Patellar (L4)
- Sensation L1-S2 to light touch
- Strength L1-S1
- Muscle strength, bulk, tone
- Babinski, clonus, modified Spurling’s
- Lhermitte’s sign

**Posterior Seated**

- Observe: posture, spinal curves
- Palpate/ROM: paraspinals, TART, AROM
- Special Tests: step off, spinous process percussion, CVA tenderness
- OSE: seated flexion test, lumbar SD, sacral sulcus

**Supine (HOB elevated to 45 degrees for patient in our case)**

- Abdominal exam: BS, palpation
- Special Tests: FABERE, SLR, Thomas test
- OSE: Mitchell Model (must palpate behind patient for sacral, PSIS, lumbar findings - patient cannot lie on stomach), Zink screen, lower crossed considerations (hypertonic adductors, piriformis, iliopsoas, erector spinae), anterior/posterior lumbar and pelvis counterstrain point palpation, lower extremity assessment (feet: arches, ankles: dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, knees: IR/ER, fibular head, femoroacetabular motion), Hip IR/ER

**Prone (not done for patient in our case)**

- Palpation: paraspinals, multifidus, quadratus lumborum, long dorsal ligament, iliolumbar ligament, piriformis, gluteus maximus, gluteus medius (look for posterior pelvis and lumbar counterstrain points, trigger points, hypertonicity, reproduction of pain symptoms)
- OSE: confirmation of sacral findings (and ILA)/PSIS findings

NOTE: Correction from this video: The **Babinski Reflex** is part of the routine neurological examination to evaluate the integrity of the corticospinal tract. In general, it is not recommended to say it is "Positive" or "Negative".  Rather, the Babinski is either **Upgoing or Downgoing.** An upgoing response is normal in infants, and present in certain medical conditions.  A downgoing response is considered normal for those over 1 year old.
