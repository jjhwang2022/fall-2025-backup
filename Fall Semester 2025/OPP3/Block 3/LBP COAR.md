- Palpate/ROM: paraspinals, TART, AROM

**Supine (HOB elevated to 45 degrees for patient in our case)**

- Special Tests: FABERE, SLR, Thomas test

- Palpation: paraspinals, multifidus, quadratus lumborum, long dorsal ligament, iliolumbar ligament, piriformis, gluteus maximus, gluteus medius (look for posterior pelvis and lumbar counterstrain points, trigger points, hypertonicity, reproduction of pain symptoms)

# Assessment - Ruling Out Emergencies and Need for Imaging

### **Ruling Out Emergencies First**

Before generating a differential diagnosis, **the physician’s first priority is to rule out emergent or serious causes** of low back pain that require immediate imaging or intervention.

These include:

**Cauda Equina Syndrome**

- _“Have you had any trouble controlling your urine or bowels?”_
- _“Have you noticed any numbness between your inner thighs or around your genitals (saddle area)?”_
- _“Have you had any new weakness in both legs or difficulty walking?”_

**Next step:**  
→ **Urgent MRI of the lumbar spine** and **immediate neurosurgical consultation**.  
Do not delay imaging—this is a surgical emergency.

**Spinal Infection**

- _“Have you had a fever, chills, or felt generally unwell?”_
- _“Have you had any recent infections, procedures, or IV drug use?”_
- _“Are you taking any medicines that weaken your immune system?”_

**Next step:**  
→ **Order CBC, ESR, CRP** to assess for systemic inflammation.  
→ **MRI with contrast** if infection suspected.  
→ **Blood cultures** ± **urgent hospital evaluation** if febrile or systemically ill.

**Malignancy or Metastatic Disease**

- _“Have you ever been diagnosed with cancer?”_
- _“Have you lost weight without trying?”_
- _“Do you have back pain that wakes you up at night or is getting worse despite rest?”_

**Next step:**  
→ **MRI of the lumbar spine** to evaluate for metastasis.  
→ **Consider ESR, CBC, and basic metabolic panel (calcium, ALP)**.  
→ Coordinate with **oncology or primary team** if history of malignancy.

**Fracture**

- _“Have you had any recent falls, accidents, or injuries?”_
- _“Do you have osteoporosis or take steroid medications?”_

**Next step:**  
→ **Plain X-ray of lumbar spine** to assess for compression or traumatic fracture.  
→ If high suspicion and X-ray inconclusive → **CT** or **MRI** for further evaluation.

**Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)**

- _“Do you feel any pulsating sensation in your abdomen?”_
- _“Have you ever been told you have aneurysms or blood vessel disease?”_
- _“Have you had sudden, severe back or abdominal pain different from your usual pain?”_

**Next step:**  
→ **Immediate abdominal ultrasound** (bedside if unstable) or **CT angiography** if stable.  
→ **Emergency vascular surgery consult** if aneurysm or rupture suspected.

# Ruling out pelvic/visceral emergencies with LBP

Our patient is cis Male, which mean there are many diagnosis in our differential that can be ruled out that involve "female" reproductive organs (i.e. uterus, ovaries, vagina).

But for patients with these reproductive organs (of any gender), there is a whole other list of diagnosis to consider, including the following Point of Care tests in clinic _regardless of history_: 

- urine HCG (every patient with ovaries, uterus, and vaginas should be tested for pregnancy, regardless of gender or sexual orientation).  
- In house UA- this is a very useful test that can be evaluated prior to seeing the patient. (This can be considered for all patients)

**Sensitivity for our gender diverse patients:** Many gender diverse patients experience gender dysphoria when their reproductive organs are being evaluated _in the context of gender_. Therefore, as providers we can approach patients with the importance of evaluating all organs inside their body _while removing the context of gender_. Ex: "_In patients with a uterus that bleeds, I'll need to ask you some questions about the bleeding to rule out some causes. Is that OK_?"

**Gynecologic Emergency – Ectopic Pregnancy**

Ask in **reproductive-age** patients (even if they deny possibility of pregnancy):

- _“When was your last menstrual period?”_
- _“Could you be pregnant?”_
- _“Have you had severe pelvic or lower abdominal pain on one side?”_
- _“Any vaginal bleeding?”_
- _"Has anyone ever hurt you physically or touched you sexually without your permission?”_

**Next step:**

→ **Urgent pregnancy test** (urine or serum hCG)  
→ Pelvic **ultrasound** and **OB-GYN** consult if positive or suspicion remains high“

→ If acute trauma secondary to abuse is suspected, refer to authorities and ER eval immediately. 

**Ovarian Pathology – Torsion, Cyst Rupture**

- _“Is your pain sharp and sudden, worse with movement?”_
- _“Any pelvic fullness, nausea, or pain with intercourse?”_
- _“Any known ovarian cysts?”_

**Next step:**  
→ Pelvic **ultrasound with Doppler**

**Pelvic Floor Dysfunction / Endometriosis → Chronic LBP Mimics**

_(Not emergent, but should be screened for in recurrent or unexplained LBP)_

- _“Do your symptoms worsen during your menstrual cycle?”_
- _“Any pain with intercourse, urination, or bowel movements?”_

**Next step:**  
→ Outpatient referral (pelvic health PT, gynecology, or pelvic pain specialist)

Low back pain can be differentiated into mechanical pain, non-mechanical pain, visceral disease, or a combination of multiple factors.  It is important to consider all three main categories when evaluating back pain. Red flags should make you consider non mechanical causes of pain.  A thorough history and physical exam should allow you to make most of the other diagnoses without the need for imaging.

Cauda equina syndrome is present for 0.04% of patients who present to primary care with low back pain.  However, it is an emergency and should be screened for.  Remember, urinary retention is an early sign and urinary and bowel incontinence is a later symptom.

The vast majority of those who present with low back pain in primary care (>85%) have nonspecific low back pain that is often musculoskeletal.  Most of these patients will improve within a few weeks.  Their pain can often be improved and their recovery hastened by osteopathic treatment.

# Assessment - Clinical Reasoning on a SOAP note

In clinic, it is going to be important you assimilate all of the information and write it correctly on your SOAP note. You want to be able to not only **verbalize your clinical reasoning, but also have the correct diagnoses**. Using the EMR, diagnoses are listed as "ICD-10 codes". This is for standardization and billing. Note! Osteopathic diagnostics are by region not somatic dysfunction!

# Anatomy

**Anatomic Considerations**

The anatomy of the back has great clinical importance as it includes the skin and subcutaneous tissue of the posterior trunk, the fascial layers, vertebral column/sacrum and the intrinsic musculature. 
![[Pasted image 20251112084326.png]]
Recall that the musculature of the back is arranged in two layers:

1) **superficial muscles** (trapezius, latissimus, rhomboids, ect.) are derived from the mesenchyme of the upper extremity and innervated by cranial nerves or ventral rami. These will not be discussed in this session as they are considered upper extremity muscles.

2) **deep back muscles** form a distinct column from cranium to sacrum and are composed of an interwoven group of extensors and stabilizers of the spine. These muscles are categorized as epaxial, and develop posterior to the transverse process and innervated by the dorsal rami. 

**Osteology of the lumbar spine**

The lumbar vertebral column consists of 5 blocky vertebrae that support the weight of the upper torso and upper extremity. Although the lumbar spine has a significant range of motion in the sagittal plane (flexion and extension) it is severely restricted in the axial plane (rotation) and has only moderate lateral flexion (side-bending).
![[Pasted image 20251112084333.png]]
Characteristics of the lumbar spine:

**Body:** Anteriorly the typical vertebra consists of a large bony oval shaped body. Both ends of the body have a ring of thickened bone around their edge termed the ring apophysis. This arrangement leaves the center of the end plate of the vertebra slightly indented from the ring on the margin of the end plate. It is into this indented area that the end-plate cartilage is located. The large oval shaped vertebral bodies and the massive zygapophysial joints are unique to the lumbar vertebrae. Each joint has its articular face approximately in the sagittal plane thus the joints allow easy movement in that plane but restrict movement in all other planes.

**Pedicles:** The posterior position of each vertebral body gives rise to two thick pedicles. Each pedicle is a thick stalk that unites with the posteriorly positioned lamina. The pedicles arise from the superior aspect of the posterior wall of the vertebra and form the lateral walls of the spinal canal.
![[Pasted image 20251112084338.png]]
**Laminae:** The roof of the spinal canal is formed by a bony plate termed a lamina. Each lamina attaches to the two pedicles anteriorly and gives rise to two superior and two inferior articular processes, left and right transverse processes laterally and a spinous process posteriorly.

**Articular processes:** Each vertebra has four articular processes, two superior and two inferior. The articular processes arise from the margins of the lamina. The inferior processes of one vertebra articulate with the superior processes of the vertebra below. Since the pedicle, which is supporting the lamina, is positioned on the superior portion of the vertebral body, the lamina and the inferior articular processes create an arch extending downward to the superior articular process below. When articulated with the superior process, the arch converts into a tunnel termed the intervertebral canal through which the spinal nerve will travel.
![[Pasted image 20251112084344.png]]
**Transverse process:** A lateral extension of the lamina, the transverse process, is present on each vertebra, however it varies in size considerably from region to region. In the lumbar region, the costal process is large and is commonly termed the "transverse process". The true transverse process, typically termed accessory process, is very small, forming a tubercle on the posterior margin of the costal process. In the sacrum, the costal and transverse processes combine to form the ala of the sacrum.  
  
**Spinous process:** The spinous process arises from the center of the lamina and projects posteriorly. In the lumbar region the spinous process is paddle-shaped and projects posteriorly. In the sacrum the spinous processes are small, elongated tubercles on the roof plate.

**Ligaments of the Lumbar Spine**

The vertebral column is supported by many strong ligaments.  Some of these ligaments span the entire length of the spinal column, and others are segmental between each vertebrae. Important ligaments include:

**Anterior longitudinal ligament:** The ALL is a broad flat band of collagenous fibers that covers the anterior aspect of the lumbar spine. It is most firmly attached to the bodies of the lumbar vertebra and less firmly to the intervertebral discs. Superiorly the ALL narrows to a point that attaches to the anterior tubercle of the anterior arch of C1 (atlas) and blends with the anterior atlantooccipital membrane. Inferiorly the ligament is broadest and curves over the sacral promontory and attaches to the ventral surface of the sacrum. This tough ligament provides the primary guard against hyperextension. The ligament receives a sensory innervation from small nerves branching off of the sympathetic trunk and forming a plexus on the ALL.
![[Pasted image 20251112084352.png]] 
**Posterior longitudinal ligament:** The PLL is a thin ligament that lies on the posterior aspect of the bodies of the lumbar vertebra inside the spinal canal. At each level, the ligament expands laterally as it firmly anchors into the lumbar intervertebral discs and then narrows as it arches over the bodies. This gives the PLL a scalloped or dentate shape. Throughout the entire length of the PLL the ventral surface of the dura shares a loose attachment.  
  
**Ligamenta Flava:** The flaval ligament forms the posterior wall of the spinal canal. This elastic ligament extends between the laminae of each vertebra. The ligament attaches to the undersurface of the lamina above and to the superior lip of the lamina below. These ligaments are found from the axis (C2) to the first sacral vertebra. In neutral, upright posture, the flaval ligament is under tension. The large amount of elastin present in the ligament helps when extending from a flexed posture and it also ensures that the ligament will not buckle into the vertebral canal on extreme extension. The lateral border of the FL forms the medial aspect of the zygapophyseal joint capsules while the medial border of the ligament blends into the interspinous ligament.  
![[Pasted image 20251112084404.png]]

**Zygapophyseal joint capsule:** Each of the z-joints in the lumbar spine is covered with a dense connective tissue capsule. The medial aspect of the capsule is derived from the lateral margin of the flaval ligament. View from behind the capsule appears to have horizontally orient collagenous fibers protecting the two articular processes from separating or gapping during rotation.

  
**Interspinous and supraspinous ligaments:**

The interspinous ligament is one of the most misrepresented ligaments in the body. It can be divided into a ventral, middle and a dorsal portion: The ventral part is a small extension of the flaval ligament onto the inferior margin of the spinous process above. The middle portion is the largest and forms an s-shaped curve from the anterior end of the superior margin of the process above.  Many of the fibers in the middle portion of the ligament are running parallel to the spinous processes rather than at 90 degrees to them and most texts depict. The dorsal portion arises from the posterior portion of the superior margin of the spinous process and fuses to the underside of the supraspinous ligament. The interspinous ligament is composed of collagen fibers, but elastic fibers become much more dense the closer it gets to the flaval ligament. This parallel array of collagen in the middle portion supports the biomechanical finding that this ligament does not offer resistance to flexion as is often proposed.   
  
**Anulus fibrosus of the intervertebral disc:** The annulus fibrosus has an outer layer that is collagenous and an inner area that is composed of fibrocartilage. The arrangement of the fibers, crossing each other obliquely limits rotation of the IV disc.  The posterior fibers are commonly parallel, which could predispose individuals to herniations.

_Spinotransversales and suboccipital groups respectively are only present in the cervical region and will not be discussed in this section._  For each of the following muscles review the origin, insertion and function of each. The general function is given in parentheses.
![[Pasted image 20251112084415.png]]
**Erector spinae: iliocostalis, longissimus, spinalis (extend back, lateral side bend)**
![[Pasted image 20251112084420.png]]
This is a posterior view of the back illustrating the muscles present in the **erector spinae group**. These muscles include the: iliocostalis (lumborum, thoracis and cervicis), the longissimus (thoracis, cervicis and capitis) and spinalis (thoracis and cervicis). Note that the iliocostalis does not reach to the cranium and that longissimus technically does not have a lumbar component. In truth, the longissimus and the iliocostalis fuse in the mid-lumbar region.

**Transversospinalis: semispinalis, multifidus, rotatores (extend back, rotate vertebral column)**
![[Pasted image 20251112084427.png]]
Semispinalis has a thoracic (thoracis), cervical (cervicis) and cranial (capitis) portion. The muscle is not represented in the lumbar region.

The multifidus has a lumbar (lumborum), thoracic (thoracis), and cervical (cervicis) portion. However, this muscle does not extend to the cranium.

Rotatores are present in the thoracic (thoracis) and cervical (cervicis) regions but are fused with the deep aspect of the multifidus muscle in the lumbar region. Two forms of rotatores are present in the thoracic and cervical regions: longus (spans two segments) and brevis (spans one segment.

**Segmental muscles: intertransversarii (postural), interspinales (postural), levator costarum (elevate ribs)**

Three muscles are present in this group: the interspinales, the intertransversarii. and the levator costarum. Interspinales are present at every level but are seen most clearly in the cervical region. Conversely, the intertransversarii are present only in the cervical and lumbar regions and are most prominent in the lumbar region. Notably, in the lumbar region there are medial and lateral intertransversarii. The medial ones are paraspinal muscles and innervated by the dorsal ramus whilst the lateral muscles are two in number - anterior and posterior - and represent intercostal muscles, both being innervated by the ventral ramus. The levator costarum arise from the transverse process of C7-T11 and run inferiorly and laterally to attach to the outer surface of the rib. The levator costarum has a role in forced inspiration. 

**Innervation of the lumbar spine**

**The spinal nerve**

**!![[Pasted image 20251112084434.png]]
The spinal nerve is a product of the fusion of the dorsal root with the ventral root; this event occurs just distal to the dorsal root ganglia. Almost immediately the spinal nerve divides into a dorsal and ventral ramus. The ventral ramus innervates the hypaxial muscles and the extremity muscles while the dorsal ramus serves to innervate the epaxial muscles.

In the lumbar region, dorsal ramus typically forms three branches: medial, intermediate and lateral. **In general, the lateral branch innervates the iliocostalis muscle, the intermediate branch innervates the longissimus muscles and the medial branch innervates all else**. In essence the medial branch innervates the muscles located between the articular processes and the spinous processes. In addition, the medial branch innervates the zygapophysial joints.

**Lumbosacral Nerve Roots, Dermatomes, and In Offcie Exams**

Low back pain can arise from irritation or compression of spinal nerve roots, particularly between **L4 and S1**, where the lumbosacral plexus transmits both motor and sensory fibers to the lower extremity. Understanding these relationships helps you correlate **neurologic findings** with anatomy and identify when pain is **radicular** rather than purely musculoskeletal.

**Lumbar and Sacral Nerve Roots**

The **lumbosacral plexus** is formed from the **ventral rami of T12–S4**.

- The **lumbar plexus (L1–L4)** supplies the anterior and medial thigh.
- The **sacral plexus (L4–S4)** supplies the posterior thigh, leg, and foot.
- Green in the figure represents **posterior divisions**, which generally supply **extensor** muscle groups.
- Yellow represents **anterior divisions**, which supply **flexor** muscle groups.

These mixed sensory and motor nerves include the **femoral**, **obturator**, **lateral femoral cutaneous**, and **sciatic** nerves—all clinically relevant in low back pain evaluation.

**Dermatomes and Sensory Distribution**

The second figure shows the **sensory map (dermatomes)** of the lower limb.

- **L1–L2:** Upper thigh and groin region
- **L3:** Anterior thigh and medial knee
- **L4:** Medial leg and ankle (“down to the floor”)
- **L5:** Dorsum of the foot and great toe
- **S1:** Lateral foot and heel
- **S2–S4:** Posterior thigh, perineum (“saddle region”)

Dermatomal testing is performed using **light touch or pinprick** to compare sides. Sensory loss following one of these patterns may indicate a **radiculopathy**.

**Clinical Integration**

- **Radiculopathy:** Nerve root irritation producing pain, numbness, or weakness along a **dermatomal pattern** (e.g., L5 pain down lateral leg into big toe).
- **Somatic dysfunction:** Pain and restriction **without** neurologic deficit; often coexists and can exacerbate radicular irritation.
- **OMT focus:** Address mechanical strain patterns (e.g., lumbar, pelvic, and sacral dysfunction) that may alter nerve root tension or local inflammation.

**Recurrent meningeal and somato-sympathetic nerves**

Two additional nerves other than the dorsal rami proper are responsible for the innervation of spinal structures. The recurrent meningeal nerve exits directly from the spinal nerve itself and re-enters the IV foramen to innervate the posterior longitudinal ligament and posterior annulus fibrosus.  The somato-sympathetic nerve exits from the grey rami communicates or from the sympathetic ganglion itself. This nerve also recurs through the IV foramen to innervate the anterior longitudinal ligament and the anterior annulus fibrosus.

**Cluneal nerves:**

The lateral branches of L1-L3 pass through the iliocostalis lumborum to become cutaneous (sensory). These nerves turn inferiorly and course downward over the superior buttock forming a series of superior cluneal nerves. The lateral branches of the S1-3 pass through the gluteus maximus to provide cutaneous innervation to the center of the buttock. The inferior cluneal nerves are a branch of the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve (ventral rami) and provides sensory innervation to the inferior portion of  the buttock.

**The Thoracolumbar Fascia**

![[Pasted image 20251112084457.png]]
The thoracolumbar fascia envelops the deep musculature of the back and is continuous from cranium to sacrum.  In the cervical region it is continuous with the deep cervical fascia and as it moves inferiorly it becomes a thin membrane over the muscles separating them from the superficial back muscles.

In the lumbar spine, the thoracolumbar fascia is organized into three layers.

- The posterior layer is attached to the spinous processes of and the spines of the sacrum.
- The middle layer is attached to the transverse processes, the iliac crest, and the lower border of the 12th rib.
- The anterior layer covers the quadratus lumborum and attaches to the transverse processes behind the psoas, the iliolumbar ligament and forms the lateral arcuate ligament of the diaphragm.

Laterally, these three layers join to form the insertion of the transversus abdominis and internal oblique muscles.  This structure forms a compartment for the erector spinae and multifidus muscles.

This fascia, where it is continuous with the abdominal wall, is important in lifting and load transfer between the trunk and the limbs. The thoracolumbar fascia is also well innervated by nociceptive nerve fibers and could play a role in some back pain.

# Osteopathic Considerations (ABCS)

Remember that we treat _people_ in osteopathic medicine, not clinical conditions. Therefore, there is no specific technique for a specific diagnosis including low back pain. Each patient, regardless of the clinical diagnosis requires a structural diagnosis in order to determine what treatment best suits their clinical presentation.  We can, however, consider each patient in the context of the A (Autonomics), B (Biomechanics), C (Circulation), S (Screening) as a way to begin to discover where we may want to aim our diagnosis and treatment.

**Required further reading: Foundations Of Osteopathic Medicine 4rd edition-Chapter 42 Acute Low Back Pain pgs 1112-1124**

- **A (Autonomics)**
    - **_Parasympathetics_**
        - None specifically for the back- but remember that decreasing sympathetic tone can assist in managing the pain response
    - **_Sympathetics_**
        - Originate at T12-L2 and innervate the anterior longitudinal ligament, anterior disc and vertebrae via somatosympathetics and dorsal structures via dorsal rami

- **B (Biomechanics)**
    - **_Lumbar Spine -_** Type I & II dysfunction
    - **_Sacrum/Pelvis_** - Mitchell Model- (dysfunctions here may alter gait)
    - **_Lower extremity_** -ankle, knee, hip biomechanics (hip/ankle to be covered next semester).
    - Articular, muscular, ligamentous, fascial restrictions from the lower extremity, pelvis, lumbar and thoracic region which may all play a role in low back pain

- **C (Circulation)**
    - **_Batson’s Plexus_**- valveless veins that connect the deep pelvic veins and thoracic veins. They drain venous blood from the spine and have connections from the pelvis into the cranium. Somatic dysfunction may impede this process.
    - **_Thoracic inlet_** - terminal lymphatic drainage may be affected here
    - **_Diaphragm_** - major lymph/venous pump
    - **_Aortic conditions_** - Be sure to screen for AAA and aortic dissection in you history
    - **_Pelvic Congestion Syndrome -_** This condition involves varicose veins in the pelvis and can cause pelvic pressure and LBP. 

- **S (Screening)**
    - **_Postural issue_** - How people sit, stand, and their gait affects low back pain which is often influenced by daily microtraumas that add up. Is there a scoliosis or spondylolisthesis? Is the patient morbidly obese (anterior fascial pull)? What do they do for work or jobs they perform at home (the mom who always lifts her child up on one hip)? What chronic movement patterns may play a role in how the mechanics of the back functions?

**Sacral Decompression**

![[Pasted image 20251112085408.png]]
**Possible Clinical Indications**: Low back pain, sacral dysfunction, pelvic tension, somatic dysfunction at the lumbosacral junction

**Possible TART Findings/Diagnosis**: Tension or asymmetry at the lumbosacral junction; restricted motion between sacrum and L5

**Example Diagnosis**: Sacral base posterior or sacral compression SD

**Patient Position**: Supine

**Physician Position**: Seated at the side of the patient

**Treatment**:

1. The physician places both hands under the lumbosacral junction so that the fingertips align with the upper sacrum and L5.
2. The inferior (caudad) hand is slid under the sacrum from the midline for better mechanical advantage.
3. The physician gently applies a superior lift with the superior (cephalad) hand and slight inferior traction with the caudad hand to induce axial decompression between the sacrum and L5.
4. The position is held until tissue release or softening is appreciated.
5. Reevaluate the original structural diagnosis and response to treatment.

**Physician Position**: Standing on the ipsilateral side

**Treatment**:

1. The physician places the monitoring hand under the lumbar paraspinals at the junctional level of dysfunction.
2. With the operating arm or torso, the physician grasps the patient's ipsilateral leg and applies gentle axial compression through the femur toward the monitoring hand.
3. Once a softening is felt in the monitored tissues, the physician instructs the patient to actively straighten the leg against resistance all the way down to the table.
4. Once the leg is on the table, the physician maintains contact and applies a gentle final tractional tug.
5. Reevaluate the original structural diagnosis and response to treatment.

**Sacroiliac (SI) Articulation – Supine**

**Possible Clinical Indications**: Sacroiliac dysfunction, pelvic asymmetry, low back pain

**Possible TART Findings/Diagnosis**: Restricted SI joint motion, asymmetrical pelvic motion

**Example Diagnosis**: Left SI joint motion restriction

**Patient Position**: Supine

**Physician Position**: Standing on the contralateral side of the dysfunction

**Treatment**:

1. The physician stabilizes the ipsilateral ASIS with one hand.
2. The other hand grasps the patient’s ipsilateral leg at the knee to control hip motion.
3. The physician induces hip flexion and applies gentle axial pressure through the femur to load the SI joint.
4. While maintaining axial load, the leg is arced in a smooth circular motion from:

- Adduction/internal rotation
- Through abduction/external rotation
- Into extension as the leg slides down the table

6. A gentle rhythmic motion is repeated 2–3 times.
7. Reevaluate the original structural diagnosis and response to treatment.

**!![[Pasted image 20251112085422.png]]

**Possible Clinical Indications**: Sacroiliac dysfunction, pelvic asymmetry, low back pain

**Possible TART Findings/Diagnosis**: Restricted SI joint mobility, somatic dysfunction of sacrum or innominate

**Example Diagnosis**: Right SI joint motion restriction

**Patient Position**: Side-lying, dysfunctional side up

**Physician Position**: Standing behind the patient

**Treatment**:

1. The physician places the posterior (monitoring) hand at the sacrum/SI joint to stabilize and palpate for motion.
2. The anterior (operating) hand contacts the patient’s top leg to control motion.

4. The top leg is moved in a smooth arc, beginning with:

- Adduction/internal rotation
- Sweeping through abduction/external rotation
- Ending in extension as the leg moves down to the table

6. The compression vector is maintained throughout. Repeat 2–3 times.
7. Reevaluate the original structural diagnosis and response to treatment.

# Plan - Pharmacology

![[Pasted image 20251112090011.png]]
#### **Acute Low Back Pain (<4 weeks)**

If the patient has not improved with **heat, OMT, massage, or acupuncture**, consider a **time-limited** trial of:

1. **NSAIDs** – _First-line pharmacologic therapy_
    
    - Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration.
        
    - **Caution:** GI bleeding/ulcer risk, renal dysfunction, cardiovascular disease or heart failure.
        
2. **Acetaminophen** – _Alternative when NSAIDs contraindicated_
    
    - Limited efficacy as monotherapy but may be used adjunctively.
        
    - **Caution:** Liver disease, heavy alcohol use, or cumulative daily dosing >3 g.
        
3. **Skeletal Muscle Relaxants** – _Short-term (≤1–2 weeks) adjunct_
    
    - May improve sleep and reduce spasm; evidence modest.
        
    - **Caution:** Sedation, impaired driving, interaction with SSRI/SNRI, avoid in older adults.
        

Current guidelines (ACP 2017; CDC 2022; VA/DoD 2023) de-emphasize routine medication use—focus first on **activity, reassurance, and manual therapy.**

#### **Chronic Low Back Pain (>12 weeks)**

If non-pharmacologic management (exercise, OMT, CBT, MBSR, PT) is insufficient:

1. **NSAIDs** – continue as first-line, intermittent use preferred.
    
2. **Duloxetine** – _Second-line_; may help with chronic or centralized pain.
    
3. **Tramadol** – _Reserved, short-term bridge_ if NSAIDs and duloxetine ineffective.
    
4. **Opioids** – _Last resort_ under CDC 2022 guidelines:
    
    - Only if benefits > risks, use lowest effective dose, and combine with functional goals.
        
5. **Gabapentin / Pregabalin** – Consider _only_ for confirmed radicular pain; evidence limited.

#### **For Our Case:**

- **Shared decision-making:** Discuss patient preferences after education on natural course and risks/benefits.
    
- **Reasonable plan:** Trial of **NSAID** (e.g., naproxen or ibuprofen) as tolerated for 5–7 days, alongside **OMT**, gentle mobility, and heat.
    
- **No indication for opioids or neuropathic agents** at this stage.

# Plan - Conservative Treatments

**Conservative Treatment (Non-Pharmacologic & Non-Surgical)**

**!![[Pasted image 20251112090034.png]]
**What does “conservative treatment” mean?**

Conservative care focuses on **non-invasive ways to help the body heal and restore function**.  
It’s the **first-line approach** for almost all cases of uncomplicated low back pain.  
The goal is to **stay active, manage pain safely, and prevent chronicity**—not to chase zero pain on day one.

- Most people improve within **4–6 weeks** without surgery or imaging.
- Early imaging, prolonged rest, or opioids can **delay recovery**.
- Knowing what counts as _conservative care_ helps you later recognize when someone has truly **“failed conservative treatment.”**

**Key Parts of Conservative Treatment (other than OMT)**

1. **Keep Moving**

- - Stay active within pain limits—**avoid bed rest**.
    - Light exercise: walking, stretching, gentle core work.
    - Movement therapies: **yoga, Pilates, tai chi**.

2. **Symptom Relief**

- - **Heat or ice** for comfort.
    - **Massage or myofascial work** to ease spasm and improve circulation.

3. **Body-Based & Complementary Care**

- - **Physical therapy** for posture and strengthening.
    - **Acupuncture** for pain modulation.
    - **Manual therapy (OMT, chiropractic, massage)** as an adjunct to active care.

4. **Mind–Body & Cognitive Care**

- - **Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)** to reduce fear-avoidance.
    - **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)** to decrease pain perception.
        - _Cherkin et al., JAMA 2016; 315(12):1240–1249_
    - Manage **sleep, mood, and stress**—these strongly affect pain.

5. **Education & Self-Care (more to this later)**

- - Explain the **natural healing timeline**.
    - Give patients resources:
        - _Choosing Wisely_ “Imaging for Back Pain” sheet
        - _Doc Mike Evans_ YouTube video: _Back Pain Explained_
        - Self-care books to keep them engaged and active.

**When Has Conservative Treatment “Failed”?**

Old language uses “failure of conservation treatment”, but it’s encouraged to use new language such as: **_Persistent symptoms after an adequate trial of conservative management_**

This means:

- Pain remains **function-limiting** after **6–8 weeks** of appropriate conservative care **and**
- The patient **adhered** to activity, stretching, and education plan.

At that point, consider:

- Imaging or further diagnostic work-up
- Injections (epidural or facet) for radicular pain
- Surgical referral only for **neurologic deficit, instability, or emergent causes**

# Plan - Lifestyle and Prevention

Lifestyle and prevention are forms of treatment.
![[Pasted image 20251112090058.png]]
By helping patients identify and address their modifiable risk factors—physically, behaviorally, and osteopathically—you support their autonomy and long-term spinal health.

Behavior modification requires a different skill set than prescribing medication or performing manipulation.

Some patients are **ready and motivated** to make changes; others may be **ambivalent or limited by their environment**.  
When exploring behavior change, assess:

- **Social determinants of health:** e.g., access to exercise spaces, safe housing, finances, stress load.
- **Readiness to change:** Is the patient contemplating, preparing, or already acting on a new habit?

Remember—education and prevention apply to _every_ patient, not just those with low back pain.

**Common Risk Factors for Low Back Pain that open Opportunities for Education and Lifestyle Change**

- Smoking
- Obesity / overweight
- Age (older)
- Female gender (speculative)
- Physically strenuous work
- Sedentary work
- Psychologically stressful work
- Low educational attainment
- Workers’ compensation insurance
- Job dissatisfaction
- Psychological contributors: somatization, anxiety, depression

**Our Patient’s Modifiable Factors**

- **Overweight:** counsel on how excess abdominal mass increases anterior load and spinal strain.
- **Smoking:** impairs disc and bone nutrition; discuss cessation resources.
- **Sedentary work:** encourage micro-breaks, stretching, and postural variation.

Originally we didn’t ask our patient about **stress at work, job satisfaction, anxiety, depression, or education level**—all of which influence pain perception and recovery.

**Home Exercise: Lower Crossed Syndrome**

Encourage simple, daily self-care exercises to reinforce treatment effects. Keeping the body in alignment

**Relax / Stretch (inhibited muscles):**

- Hip flexors (iliopsoas)
- Lumbar erector spinae
- Quadratus lumborum
- Piriformis

**Strengthen / Activate (weakened muscles):**

- Gluteus maximus
- Gluteus medius
- Transversus abdominis (TA)
- Abdominals and deep core stabilizers
