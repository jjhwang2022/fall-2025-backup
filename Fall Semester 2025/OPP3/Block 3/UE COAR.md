**Subacromial Impingement Syndrome and related issues-UptoDate**

Rehabilitation of any injury requires a specific plan and exercise progression. With shoulder impingement syndrome (SIS), there are four primary goals of rehabilitation [[3,17-19Links to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/rehabilitation-principles-and-practice-for-shoulder-impingement-and-related-problems/abstract/3,17-19)]:

●Strengthen the muscles that stabilize the scapula: By strengthening the scapular stabilizers, greater stability is provided for the rotator cuff muscles, which originate on the scapula. This stability allows for greater efficiency and muscular endurance of the rotator cuff and improved overall shoulder function. This is a critical first step in rehabilitation. A subset of patients with deficiencies in scapular motion may need these addressed.

●Correct imbalances in strength and flexibility about the glenohumeral joint, including optimizing rotator cuff strength and ensuring mobility of the posterior shoulder capsule: Typically, before rehabilitation, the muscles at the front of the shoulder complex (anterior deltoid, internal rotator [ie, subscapularis]) are 1.5 to 2 times stronger than those at the posterior (posterior deltoid, external rotators).

●Stabilize the secondary movers of the shoulder complex: Once the primary muscles of the shoulder are strong and functional, the next step is to rehabilitate the secondary shoulder muscles in order to improve coordination of the entire shoulder complex.

●Correct imbalances, weaknesses, or dysfunction at any segment along the kinetic chain (ankle, knee, hip, torso, etc).

In addition to these four primary goals, a fifth goal for many athletes is to improve sport-specific biomechanics and function. This may entail performing exercises that simulate key movements in their sport using resistance or other techniques to improve performance. Proper technique is an essential component of this phase. During this final stage, it may be helpful to have a coach with sport-specific expertise assess the athlete's mechanics.

Patients should be made aware that successful completion of such a program generally requires 8 to 16 weeks, but some improvement is usually noted within the first three to four weeks. A patient who has successfully completed a rehabilitation program for SIS should have complete, pain-free motion of the glenohumeral joint and should be able to perform all functional movements and exercises in the program without pain. Several months following the completion of rehabilitation may be needed before pain during sleep completely resolves. For athletes, successful rehabilitation is generally determined by the ability to return to play at their previous level of performance.

It is important to maintain the patient's confidence during rehabilitation. If a program is too easy and provides little benefit, patient compliance may fall; if a program is too difficult, pain may increase and the patient may quit rehabilitation. To avoid such problems when rehabilitating patients with shoulder pain, it is important to distinguish between SIS and rotator cuff tears at the outset and to refer patients back to their primary care or sports medicine clinician to discuss possible surgical evaluation as soon as the need is recognized (eg, patient demonstrates significant weakness consistent with a substantial rotator cuff tear). 

**SLAP tears-UptoDate**

**Initial treatment** — Initial management of SLAP tears involves reducing pain by avoiding aggravating activities, such as overhead throwing or work where the shoulder is placed repeatedly in abducted and externally rotated positions. Nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or [acetaminophenLinks to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/acetaminophen-paracetamol-drug-information?search=Subacromial%20impingement%2C%20bursitis&topicRef=13815&source=see_link) can be taken to help reduce pain in the acute setting. Once pain is adequately controlled, patients begin a rehabilitation program with the goals of improving glenohumeral and scapulothoracic motion and increasing the strength and endurance of the rotator cuff and scapulothoracic muscles [[28Links to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/superior-labrum-anterior-to-posterior-slap-tears/abstract/28)]. 

**Nonoperative treatment** — Typically, nonoperative management of SLAP tears is preferred whenever possible given the long recovery required following surgical repair (typically 6 to 12 months) and the limitations of surgical treatment [[45Links to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/superior-labrum-anterior-to-posterior-slap-tears/abstract/45)]. Rehabilitation programs focused specifically on addressing scapular dyskinesia and posterior capsule contractures associated with glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD) enable approximately 40 percent of professional baseball players to resume playing without surgery [[46Links to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/superior-labrum-anterior-to-posterior-slap-tears/abstract/46)]. Although studies are limited primarily to retrospective case series, evidence suggests that most patients are able to resume preinjury activity levels following participation in a well-designed physical therapy program.

In a systematic review of five studies involving 244 young adult athletes with primarily type 2 SLAP tears, the return-to-play rate was approximately 50 percent overall and 76 to 78 percent among those who completed the rehabilitation protocol, which required 20 sessions on average [[47Links to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/superior-labrum-anterior-to-posterior-slap-tears/abstract/47)]. As an example of the type of study included in the review, a retrospective study of 39 patients involving a rehabilitation protocol similar to that described above for baseball players reported improved pain and quality of life scores in 71 percent of patients, and 67 percent of overhead athletes returned to their sport at preinjury levels [[48Links to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/superior-labrum-anterior-to-posterior-slap-tears/abstract/48)].

The typical approach to rehabilitation of SLAP lesions includes addressing the following principles and biomechanical deficits [[8,49,50Links to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/superior-labrum-anterior-to-posterior-slap-tears/abstract/8,49,50)]:

●Reduce pain and inflammation.

●Restore pain-free range of motion, including identifying and resolving any GIRD. Specific stretches and exercises help improve internal rotation in these patients.

●Improve function and strength of scapular stabilizers – Exercises to accomplish this goal are described separately. 

●Improve rotator cuff strength – Exercises to accomplish this goal are described separately.

# Osteopathic Considerations (ABC'S) UE COAR

- **A (Autonomics)**

- - **Sympathetics**

- Originate at T1-T4- innervation then goes to the cervical ganglia before making its way to the upper extremities

- - **_Parasympathetics_**

- Few in the extremities

- - **_Other Innervation_**

- - - Brachial plexus- Consider the innervation of the muscles of the shoulder and where those nerves may become impinged or entrapped- be sure to evaluate the cervical spine

- **B (Biomechanics)**
    - Consider that restrictions in one joint can lead to compensatory movement in other joints.  All joints of the shoulder need to move freely in order for proper functioning of the shoulder.  

- “Some factors that predispose the patient to shoulder pain include unstable glenohumeral joints, weakness of scapular stabilizers, abnormal posture, and hypomobility of the cervical and thoracic spine” (Pain Medicine & Management 2nd edition)
- Consider the importance of motion in each of the following joints:

- - - **_Sternoclavicular joint_**

- - - - - Important connection of the upper extremity to the thorax- the only true bony joint to do so. Restriction of motion here will lead to restriction of motion at the AC joint.
                - Shoulder shrug = inferior glide
                - Shoulder depression = superior glide
                - Scapular protraction = posterior glide
                - Scapular retraction = anterior glide

- - - **_Acromioclavicular joint_**

- - - - - Connects the scapula to the clavicle and motion is necessary for shoulder abduction to occur.
                - Adduction causes superior glide
                - Abduction causes inferior glide
                - Internal rotation causes anterior glide
                - External rotation causes posterior glide

- - - **_Glenohumeral joint_**

- - - - - Lots of mobility, little stability. The rotator cuff muscles work to keep it in place and stabilized. Alteration in rotator cuff function or scapular kinematics can affect this joint.
                - Commonly the posterior capsule is tight- this causes anterior translation and elevation of the humeral head which could lead to impingement syndromes.
                - Major motions: Flexion, Extension, abduction, adduction, IR, EX
                - Minor motions: superior/inferior and anterior posterior glides

- - - **_Scapulothoracic joint_**

- - - - - The scapula on the thorax- pseudoarticulation- attached by rhomboids, trapezius, serratus anterior, pectoralis minor and levator scapula.  Tension on the muscles controls the position. Changes in the position of the scapula alter the length tension relationships and positions of the rotator cuff muscles and joint arthrokinematics.
                - Motions include superior/inferior glide, rotation and protraction/retraction

- - - **_Elbow_**

- - - - Connections from the shoulder (biceps, triceps) have long connections to the elbow. Elbow biomechanics may play a role in shoulder biomechanics.
            - Consider the:
                - Radiohumeral joint (anterior/posterior glide with supination and pronation)
                - Ulnohumeral joint (abduction/adduction glide and slide)

- - - **_Thoracic spine/Ribs_**

- - - - - The scapula must glide over the thoracic spine and ribs. Somatic dysfunction of the thoracic spine may alter the position of the scapula or affect musculature connecting to the scapula.

- - - **_Cervical spine_**

- - - - - Many of the muscles that control scapular motion attach or originate from the cervical spine, therefore these areas must be assessed for somatic dysfunction.  Additionally the brachial plexus comes from C5-T1 and should be assessed for anything that may impede or compress it.

- - - **_Pelvis_**

- - - - Don’t miss long musculoskeletal and fascial connections like the latissimus dorsi which attaches onto the iliac crest and the humerus

- **C (Circulation)**

- **_Axillary region-_** lymphatics from the upper extremity flow to the axillary region before moving into the thoracic duct. Myofascial restrictions in this region, including from pectoralis major/minor may impede lymphatic flow
- **_Thoracic inlet_**- lymphatic and vascular drainage from the arm may be impeded here
- **_Diaphragm_**- major lymph/venous pump

- **S (Screening)**
- - **_Postural issue_**- forward head/neck posture, anterior shoulder, scoliosis

- - **_Upper Crossed Syndrome_**

- Typically tight:  pectoralis major, upper trapezius, levator scapulae, scalenes, sternocleidomastoid, latissimus dorsi, biceps, supscapularis
- Typically weak: triceps, rhomboids, serratus anterior, lower trapezius, supraspinatus, infraspinatus

- - **_Leg length discrepancy- or sacral base unleveling can cause compensatory postures and altered biomechanics all the way up to the thorax, ribs, cervical spine head and even upper extremity_**

- - Mind/Body/Spirit Considerations- life stressors can contribute to increase in muscle tensions in the neck and shoulder region
    - **_Ergonomics-_** how does this patient sit, stand, work, sleep

4. 3 Motions:
    1. Rotation about an A-P axis – Use to lateralize to most restricted side
        1. Created by Shoulder Abduction:(‘Normal ROM’=180 Deg.) palms facing out.
        2. Observe & palpate for asymmetry & quality of motion. The side that moves first is considered to be the most restricted- as described in prior labs. Observe all the way to the fingers for static asymmetry
            1. Superior (aka upward) rotation=glenoid fossa facing superior-Abduct arm
            2. Inferior (aka downward) rotation=glenoid fossa facing inferior- Adduct arm
    2. Elevation/Depression (Shrug shoulders, reach towards floor)
    3. Protraction/Retraction (move shoulders anteriorly & posteriorly toward midline of body)

**AC Joint**:

1. Patient seated- operator behind the patient- one hand palpates the superior aspect of the AC- the other grasps the forearm
2. Operator, while monitoring at the AC Joint:
    1. Adduction causes superior glide
    2. Abduction causes inferior glide
    3. Internal rotation causes anterior glide
    4. External rotation causes posterior glide
3. Restriction in symmetric motion denotes somatic dysfunction

**SC Joint**:

1. Observe for static positional asymmetry/deformity
2. Palpate SC joints & note Asymmetry of position, Tissue Texture, and Tenderness
3. Spring SC Joints to help lateralize: GENTLY spring proximal clavicles inferiorly, superiorly and posteriorly (segmentally diagnose and treat side with less spring first)
    1. Inferior/Superior glide (have patient shrug shoulders, then reach towards their feet)
    2. Posterior/Anterior glide (shoulder flexion to 90 deg. then reach forward) Protraction about 2-3 inches, followed by Retraction)
    3. Anterior/Posterior rotation (abduct arms to 90 deg., Internally Rotate humerus until clavicle anteriorly rotates, Externally Rotate humerus until clavicle posteriorly rotates -with elbows flexed or straight)

**Abducted/Adducted Elbow**:

1. Patient is supine and shoulder abducted to 45 degrees. Place the hand midway between supination and pronation (you can then pin it at your side). Grasp the elbow with both hands, induce slight flexion
2. Introduce a very slight medial and lateral motion and observe for ease and tension paying close attention to anatomic barriers.

**Radial head:**

2. Other hand fingers or thumb monitors radial head for response to:
    1. Supination-radial head glides anteriorly
    2. Pronation- radial head glides posteriorly

2. Start proximally on posterior (dorsal) aspect of forearm just distal to Radial head and move distally. once tissue thins out you can palpate either anterior or posterior aspects of distal forearm and reach the IOM

**1st rib FPR**

- Pt is supine- operator stands facing them- placing monitoring hand (the one nearest patient) on the posterior first rib to monitor
- With the opposite hand, the operator grasps the elbow, and abducts the arm to 90 degrees with full elbow flexion
- A compressive force is created through the elbow downward to the monitoring finger
- Operator uses their monitoring arm as a fulcrum, increasing shoulder flexion- holds for 3-5 seconds
- Maintaining the compressive force and IR, the operator adducts the upper arm across the patient’s chest and swing it down into a neutral position

**Clavicle treatment- BLT**

- The patient is seated- operator stands in a split stance in front of the patient with one hand grasping the distal clavicle and one hand grasping the proximal clavicle
- Operator then has the patient lean forward until the operator feels that they can “float” the clavicle
- The operator can modify their pressure to lift either end up or down, IR or ER, compression or distraction until a balance point of even tension is reached
- Maintain this hold until a release is felt. Can fine tune by having the patient very slowly turning their head to the left or to the right
- After the release- the operator supports the clavicle as the patient sits back up- releasing only after they are back in a normal seated posture

# Pharmacology UE COAR

Acute treatment — Research to direct management of Shoulder Impingement Syndromes is limited, but the following is a generally accepted approach for acute symptoms: per UptoDate

●Cryotherapy – Ice may decrease acute swelling and inflammation and provide some analgesia.

●Rest – This means avoiding activities that aggravate symptoms, including all overhead activities.

●Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)

For acute injuries, we give a short course (two to four weeks) of scheduled NSAID therapy. If the patient has no major contraindications to high-dose NSAIDs (eg, bleeding gastric ulcer), we often prescribe a two-week regimen (eg, [ibuprofenLinks to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/ibuprofen-drug-information?search=pharmacologic%20management%20shoulder%20syndromes&topicRef=240&source=see_link) 800 mg three times daily, [naproxenLinks to an external site.](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/naproxen-drug-information?search=pharmacologic%20management%20shoulder%20syndromes&topicRef=240&source=see_link) 500 mg twice daily). If there are relative contraindications to high-dose NSAIDs (eg, mild renal insufficiency), we may give a two-week course of an over-the-counter NSAID using a lower dose (eg, ibuprofen 200 to 400 mg). Thereafter, patients may use an NSAID as needed.
