# Differential DDx Expanded

Thinking further about our differential diagnoses:

It can be helpful to first divide conditions into acute or chronic. **Acute** knee pain is pain that lasts for less than 6 weeks, while pain lasting longer than 6 weeks is considered **chronic**. Acute pain can often be due to trauma or overuse.   It is important to determine if the onset was abrupt (i.e.  trauma) or insidious (i.e.  overuse). Some patients complain of a sudden worsening of chronic pain (acute on chronic) that may be due to overuse exacerbated by increased activity. 

Next, determine how/an acute injury has occurred.

- Direct contact (falling, direct blow to the knee, or motor vehicle accident)
- Non-contact (running, jumping, squatting, slipping, twisting) are common traumatic injuries to knees

It is helpful to know when the injury occurred, how long until the pain developed, and exactly the movement the patient was doing when the trauma occurred. 

Look to see if an effusion is present. This can be done by “milking” the knee or from visual inspection. If a traumatic injury occurred during athletics, ask if the effusion began immediately after the trauma or developed over the next day or two.

You should also ask if the patient was able to return to play. In ACL tears, for example, swelling will begin almost immediately, and the patient will not be able to return to play.

In MCL injuries, the swelling begins after 2-3 days and the athlete is likely able to return to play after the injury, sometimes during the same day.

An effusion following acute trauma is more likely to suggest structural damage to the knee such as bone, cartilage, or a ligament.  If there is an effusion present in a non-traumatic  knee, further workup is warranted (septic joint should be on your differential). 

Finally, determine the location of pain. Ask the patient to point with one finger where it hurts the most. This will drive your differential diagnosis.

## Fascia of the Lower Extremity

**Superficial layer –**

- a membranous layer of connective tissue
- continuous throughout the lower limb
- sends thin bands of connective tissue that connect
    - superficially to the epidermis
    - deep to the deep fascia layer
    - contains adipose tissue, veins, arteries and lymphatics
- very thick proximal and becomes very thin distally around the ankle and

The blood supply contained within the superficial fascia is primarily used to supply the skin and to regulate body temperature.

![[Pasted image 20251103145848.png]]
**Deep layer –**

●    thick sheet of connective tissue surrounding the entire leg  
●    stocking-like structure creating a container for the musculature and neurovasculature  
●    invest into the periosteum of the bone creating muscular-fascial compartments dividing the lower extremity into functional muscle groups

Thigh: the deep fascia is named the fascia lata, with septal bands dividing the thigh into anterior, posterior and medial compartments. The lateral thickening of this fascia lata forms the iliotibial band and is continuous with the aponeurosis of the tensor fascia lata and the gluteus maximus muscles.

Leg: the crural fascia, similar to the thigh, with septal bands dividing the leg into anterior, posterior and lateral compartments.  
This deep layer is important because it confines the musculature, making contraction more efficient, and improving venous and lymphatic return.

## Lower Extremity Development and Dermatomes

![[Pasted image 20251103145900.png]]
Upper and Lower limb development usually begins during the 4th week of development, with the upper limb buds appearing first followed by the lower limb buds several days later.  The limb buds first appear on the ventrolateral body wall initially and extend ventrally. By week 6 the limb buds expand and flatten into paddle shapes. During the seventh week of development, the upper limb rotates laterally by 90 degrees, so that the palm of the hand faces anteriorly and the thumb points laterally. In contrast, the lower limb undergoes a 90-degree medial rotation, thus bringing the big toe to the medial side of the foot.  This results in the flexor compartments of the lower limb being located posteriorly and the extensor compartments located anteriorly. Creating the non-linear appearance of the dermatomal patterns.

## Nerves of the lower extremity: Lumbosacral plexus

Three major nerves from the lumbosacral plexus service most of the lower extremity.

**Femoral nerve (L2-L4)** originates deep to the psoas major, passes deep to the inguinal ligament and enters the femoral triangle, where it splits into many branches to supply the musculature of the anterior thigh.

**Obturator nerve (L2-L4)** also has its origins on the abdomen and passes through the obturator foramen to enter the medial compartment of the thigh. Here, it splits into a superficial and deep branch to innervate the adductor muscles.

**Sciatic nerve (L4-S3)** comes together in the pelvis and descends anterior to the piriformis muscle and passes through the greater sciatic foramen to gain access to the posterior thigh muscles.

The **Sciatic Nerve** splits into:

- **Tibial Nerve (L5-S2)** - Continues inferiorly to innervate the posterior leg and plantar foot.
- **Common Fibular Nerve** **(L5-S2)** - Divides into the **Superficial Fibular Nerve**, which serves the lateral compartment of the leg and the dorsum of the foot, and the **Deep Fibular Nerve** that serves that anterior compartment of the leg.

## The Knee Joint

The knee joint is the largest synovial joint in the body and comprises three distinct joints within a single joint capsule. This joint capsule is composed of an external fibrous layer, an internal synovial layer with attachments to the femur superiorly, the tibia inferiorly and posteriorly encloses the condyles. The joint capsule is continuous with the deep fascia of the lower extremity.

#### **Knee ligaments**

Surrounding the knee joint are a large complex of ligaments. Some lie outside of and are continuous with the joint capsule and others are outside of the capsule. Briefly discuss the functions of the following ligaments.

**Extracapsula****r**

- Patellar Ligament - Continuous with quadriceps tendon and knee capsule, knee extension.
- Fibular (Lateral) Collateral Ligament - Cord-like, lateral epicondyle of femur to fibular head. Knee  
    stabilization.
- Tibial (Medial) Collateral Ligament - Flat, band-shaped, medial epicondyle of femur to medial condyle of  
    tibia. Knee stabilization.
- Oblique Popliteal Ligament - An expansion of the semimembranosus muscle, running from the  
    medial tibial condyle to the lateral femoral condyle. Reinforces the joint capsule, and prevents  
    hyperextension and external rotation of the knee.
- Arcuate Popliteal Ligament - Fibular head to superomedial posterior knee joint capsule.  
    Posterior stability of the knee joint and capsule.

**Intracapsular**

- Anterior Cruciate Ligament - Limits posterior rolling of femur on the tibia and hyperextension of  
    the knee.
- Posterior Cruciate Ligament - Prevents hyperflexion of the knee and limit anterior movement of  
    the femur on the tibia.
- Lateral/Medial Meniscus - Fibrocartilaginous extensions of the tibial condyles meant to deepen  
    the joint and play a role in shock absorption.

## Knee arthrokinematics, the “screw home mechanism” and the role of popliteus.

**Open kinetic chain (swing phase):**

- During **knee extension** the tibia moves anteriorly on the femur (from 20 degrees flexion to extension) and rotates externally.
- During **knee flexion** (full extension to 20 degrees flexion) the tibia moves posteriorly on the femur and rotates internally.

**Closed kinematic chain (stance phase)**:

- During **knee extension** the femur moves posteriorly on the stable tibia (from 20 degrees flexion to extension) and rotates internally.
- During **knee flexion** (full extension to 20 degrees flexion) the femur moves anteriorly on the femur and rotates externally.
![[Pasted image 20251103150059.png]]
This “screw home mechanism” is this **rotation between the tibia and the femur**. During the terminal degrees of knee extension, there is external rotation resulting in the tightening of both cruciate ligaments, locking and stabilizing the knee.

The role of the popliteus muscle is to **unlock the knee** by rotating the femur on a fixed tibia or rotating the tibia on an unfixed limb.

Altered position of the tibia can affect this mechanism and potentiate injury.

# Osteopathic Considerations (ABC'S) LE COAR

Remember that we treat _people_ as individuals, NOT clinical conditions. Therefore, there is no specific technique(s) for a specific diagnosis including knee pain. Each patient, regardless of the clinical diagnosis, requires a structural diagnosis to determine what treatment best suits their clinical presentation.

There is no algorithm for the osteopathic treatment of patients since no one person will have the same constellation of musculoskeletal abnormalities. We can, however, consider each patient in the context of the A (Autonomics), B (Biomechanics), C (Circulation), S (Screening) as a way to begin to discover where we may want to aim our diagnosis and treatment.

## A (Autonomics)

##### ○     Sympathetics

- At theT10-L2 levels the sympathetic trunk descends to the lumbar and sacral ganglia and then postganglionic nerves join the spinal nerves (ventral rami) of the lumbosacral plexus to reach the vasculature of the lower extremities

##### ○     Parasympathetics

- No real parasympathetic innervation to the extremities

## B (Biomechanics)

- Acknowledge that biomechanical alterations in joints can affect the biomechanical motions, kinetic chains, and arthrokinematics of other joints. Remember that there can be biomechanical alterations from muscle imbalance, fascial restrictions and joint restrictions.
- **_Hip-_** femoroacetabular motion (f/e/abd/adduction/IR/ER)
- **_Knee_**- tibiofemoral motion (f/e/IR/ER) as well as anterior/posterior glide (demonstrated in OPP4), fibular head motion (Anterolateral/posteromedial)
- **_Ankle-_** tibiotalar motion (plantar/dorsiflexion), talocalcaneal motion (inversion/eversion)
- **_Foot-_** Tarsal/metatarsal/phalangeal motion- to be reviewed next semester
- **_Sacrum/Pelvis_**- Mitchell Model

## C (Circulation)

- **_Popliteal fossa_**- restricted fascial or muscular tension may impede lymphatic flow from the LE
- **_Inguinal region-_**restricted fascial or muscular tension my impede lymphatic flow from the LE
- **_Thoracic inlet_**- lymphatic and vascular drainage from the knee may be impeded here (as terminal drainage)
- **_Thoracoabdominal diaphragm_**- major lymph/venous pump
- **_Pelvic diaphragm_**- restricted fascial or muscular tension my impede lymphatic flow from the LE

## S (Screening)

- **_Postural issue_**- lower crossed syndrome (sensory motor issues leading to maladaptive muscle firing patterns), overpronation/supination, gait issues, consider ergonomics in what they do for work and at home, and what kind/how old their shoes are/how many miles they have run in them
- **_Leg length discrepancy_**- sacral base unleveling can cause compensatory mechanisms
- **_Past traumas_**- ask about past physical trauma which could lead to biomechanical, autonomic, or circulatory restrictions
- **_Mind/Body/Spirit considerations_**- life stressors can contribute to pain and dysfunction and increase allostatic load. Athletes often place their sense of self in their athletic abilities.

**Tibiofemoral Motion**

- **Flexion/extension** (major motion)
    - Check as you would for the major motion except really noticing any subtle difference between sides

- **Internal/external rotation**
    - Passive
        - The patient is seated, the student doctors (SD) cephalad hand monitors the knee joint line while the caudad hand grasps the heel keeping the knee at 90 degrees
        - Compare internal and external rotation and name for the position of ease
    - Active
        - The patient is seated, SD cephalad hand lines up the second finger at the inferior aspect of the patella and the caudad hand lines up on the superior aspect of the tibial tuberosity
        - These two points should line up. If the tibial tuberosity is lateral- it may indicate ER and if it is medial, it may indicate IR. Confirm with active motion
        - Have the patient extend their knee. The tibial tuberosity should move slightly laterally. If it moves laterally or does not move at all- it is internally rotated. If it moves very far laterally, it may be externally rotated
        - Be sure to compare both sides
